Last January, I found a phrase that I'd been looking for. I had been grappling with the sensation that concepts are "clustered" together with LSD and other psychedelics, and that one concept could cause another to pop into my mind with a feeling of inevitability.

What I was searching for was this:

On January 31st, 2022, I Googled "semantic priming lsd".

My search led me [to this paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1389945721001696).

I'd like to walk you through what they mean by "semantic priming".

To do this, let's go to their Figure 1.

![image.png](https://files.mastodon.social/media_attachments/files/109/119/575/295/296/360/original/9c83d7e5490b2ec1e.png)

The authors describe their task:

> A total of 48 images of animals and objects were selected from the BOSS database (Brodeur et al., 2010) (see Supplemental Material for the full set of images).

> The images were presented individually in black and white in the center of a 1280×1024 screen on a black background.

> Participants completed the task under the influence of either LSD or a placebo (see below).

> Participants were instructed to name each of the images out loud as quickly and accurately as possible.

They're saying that they showed people pictures of things and animals and asked them to name what they saw.

> Participant responses on each trial were recorded via the microphone and coded after the experiment by two independent raters.

> The first (most common) response for each trial was scored as correct (e.g., “tiger” for the picture of a tiger) or incorrect (e.g., “lion” for the picture of a tiger).

> Incorrect responses were then further subdivided into semantic (e.g., “lion”) and non-semantic (e.g., “pen”) errors.

> Responses were scored as semantically related if they were superordinates (e.g., “animal” for the picture of a tiger), subordinates (e.g., “bengal” for the picture of a tiger), semantic associates (e.g., “stripes” for the picture of a tiger), or categorical coordinates (e.g., “lion” for the picture of a tiger) of the correct response.

> Responses that reflected the same object as the correct response but were more specific (e.g., “raven” for crow) or more general (e.g., “bird” for crow) were considered as semantic errors.

So, for example, if the researchers showed you a picture of a tiger, and you said "lion" - they'd score that as a "semantic error".

Imagine if you were doing this task while high on LSD. If you said "lion" instead of "tiger" - you might be able to understand how you got there, right?

tiger --> cat --> lion

This is what it might mean to say that psychedelics increase semantic priming.

What I'd like to do

For example, how do I know that the behavior of someone saying "lion" instead of "tiger" is the same as my sensation of concepts being "clustered" together?

How do I know that this doesn't just apply to this specific task (naming pictures presented on a screen)?

That's what I'd like to do here.
