The paper [World Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.10122) by David Ha and Jürgen Schmidhuber came out a few months ago, and since then I haven't been able to get it out of my mind. I've been following hardmaru (David Ha) since I heard about his great [sketch-rnn](https://github.com/hardmaru/sketch-rnn) model, which lets a machine draw like a human.

In this post I'll explain the World Models paper in my own words. It's a work in progress since I'm still building my own version of it. The code lives on my [World Models experiments](https://github.com/sksq96/pytorch-mdn).

### Summary

World Models is an algorithm for RL agents that lets them learn inside their own imagination, with the help of a neural network. The paper shows that an agent can train inside its own "dream" and transfer what it learned in the dream back to the real environment.

I thought the paper was hard to follow at first, and writing paper notes wasn't enough for me to really understand it. Building the model myself was a much better way to understand it. So I decided to share the process, and in future posts I'll take apart each model that makes up World Models.

### The paper

*(If you haven't seen the World Models paper yet, I suggest you read it before continuing.)*

The core idea: train an RL agent inside its own "dream" (a neural network) instead of the real environment.

![World Models overview](/writings/world-models/combined.png)

The paper has three parts:

1. V: A VAE that learns from an environment
2. M: An RNN that predicts the future of an environment
3. C: A controller for the environment that uses the VAE and the RNN

#### V: A VAE that learns from an environment

V (Variational Autoencoder) is a generative neural network. We start by giving it a frame of the environment, and V encodes it into a smaller vector `z`. This is lossy compression: `z` holds only the important parts of the frame. A vector is easier to reason about than a frame, and that's why we want `z`. We then decode `z` back into a frame.

![VAE reconstruction](/writings/world-models/vae-cnn-16.png)

The example above is from the Google CarRacing-v0 environment. It's a frame of the track that the VAE reconstructed. You can see a little lossiness in the details.

#### M: An RNN that predicts the future of an environment

M (Recurrent Neural Network) is a predictive neural network. We give it a sequence of `z` vectors and `a` vectors (`a` is the action we took). In the paper `z` has 32 dimensions and `a` has 3. M learns to predict the next `z`.

M is a Mixture Density Network (MDN-RNN). MDN-RNN can predict more than one possible outcome, which is what we want, since the "future" is never certain. The RNN part is LSTM.

![MDN-RNN](/writings/world-models/mdn-rnn-15.png)

The example above is the MDN-RNN predicting a single possible future in CarRacing-v0.

#### C: A controller (very simple NN?!)

C (Controller) is the simplest model. It's a single dense layer. In the paper C has 3 inputs and 867 parameters. C takes the current `z` and `h` as input. `z` is the VAE output for the current frame, and `h` is the RNN's hidden state. C returns the action `a` that the agent will take in the environment. The agent then interacts with the environment using `a`.

This is the most astonishing part of the paper to me. C is so simple that I couldn't believe it could act as a policy for the agent.

### MDN-RNN

The part that was hardest for me to understand was the M of World Models, the MDN-RNN. M is the neural net that predicts the next frame. So I want to explain it a little more.

From the paper, M uses a mixture density LSTM network (mdn-LSTM).

![mdn-LSTM](/writings/world-models/rnn.png)

The code below shows how the MDN-RNN predicts the next frame. `h`, `c`, `z`, `a` are its inputs. `a` is the action. `h` and `c` are the RNN cell states. `z` is the compressed image, the output of the VAE. Here's the code with some explanation of what it does:

```
h, c = self.lstm(torch.cat([z, a], dim=1), (h, c)) # (batch, HIDDEN_SIZE)
z_pi, z_mu, z_sigma = mdn(h) # (batch, MDN_NUM_MIXTURES), (batch, MDN_NUM_MIXTURES, Z_SIZE), (batch, MDN_NUM_MIXTURES, Z_SIZE)
```

1. Concatenate `z` and `a`, then feed them into the LSTM. The LSTM returns hidden states `h` and `c`.
2. Feed `h` into `mdn`. The `mdn` function computes `z_pi`, `z_mu`, `z_sigma`.

`mdn` is the part I didn't understand at first. It's the mixture density network that makes M predict more than one possible outcome. `mdn` takes a 512-dimensional vector and returns `z_pi` (5), `z_mu` (5, 32), and `z_sigma` (5, 32). `z_mu` and `z_sigma` describe 5 separate distributions, one per possible future. `z_pi` is the coefficient of each distribution.

### Update

Thanks for the feedback. I realized my model wasn't working because of a simple mistake. I was scaling the images to between -1 and 1 instead of between 0 and 1. After fixing it, the VAE started predicting good frames.

Rebuilding an ML paper with your own hands is a lot of fun. If you're interested in doing something similar, here's how I went about it:

1. Read the paper a few times and get a general sense of it.
2. Write paper notes and fill in the gaps.
3. Implement the code while reading the paper.
