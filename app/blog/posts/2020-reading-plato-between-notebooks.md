Today's post is a bit of a detour from the Jupyter Notebooks, but it is still related.

In my Google searches from January I have a bunch of queries about Plato and Socrates. I was reading the Republic by Plato, and I remember being frustrated by Socrates' unwillingness to deliver a positive definition of justice. Instead he only delivers refutations of other people's definitions.

Trying to make sense of it, I 'remembered' one of the interviews from Rebecca Goldstein's 'Plato at the Googleplex'. It's a funny, clever book that I read several years ago, but I didn't remember much of it.

That's the thing about memory -- it never works like you think it should.

I found an article with a quote from the book and then watched a few YouTube videos. I ended up going back to the book, and rereading it. It is a very clever experiment, using a Socratic philosophical dialogue to probe our own times.

I was thinking about how I could connect this with other posts, and I realized that there is a connection.

I've been writing lately about how to work with ideas, and how to incubate them.

It seems that doing a deep dive into the Republic by Plato might be a good example.

My notebook entry, written on 2017-09-02:

>Glaucon puts forward the "Gyges" argument for the intrinsic superiority of injustice over justice.  Let's paraphrase his argument:
>
>1) Two men are given magical rings that render them invisible.
>2) One man is just, the other unjust.
>3) Once the rings are given, both men will act unjustly, since they can do so without fear of reprisal.
>4) Therefore, no one is just willingly.
>5) Therefore, justice is not good in itself, but only instrumentally good.
>
>Socrates will counter this argument by presenting an account of justice and demonstrating that it is good in itself.

Some notes on the Republic (from 2018-06-09):

>The Republic Book I
>
>* "How will you look for it, Socrates, when you do not know at all what it is? ... Which of the things you don't know will you set down as the thing you're looking for?"
>* "I know that I know nothing"
>* "I am not even trying to make these things clear to myself"
>
>Socrates, when challenged to define "justice", tries to deflect the challenge:
>
>>"I know that I know nothing"
>
>Isn't this a dodge?
>
>There is a difference between:
>
>* "I don't know what the answer is"
>* "I don't know how to phrase my answer"
>* "I know that I don't know the answer"
>
>The last one is not a good reason not to try to answer the question.
>
>After much back and forth, the discussion comes to a head:
>
>* Thrasymachus: "justice is nothing other than the advantage of the stronger"
>* Socrates: (tries to show that the laws that the stronger make cannot be advantageous to them)
>* Thrasymachus: (tries to redefine his definition)
>* Socrates: (rebuts the redefinition)
>
>Thrasymachus: "...you are far from realizing how much it profits one to be unjust"
>Socrates: "On the contrary, I do believe it."
>
>Glaucon: "...what sort of thing do they say justice is ..."
>
>Socrates: "... they praise justice and blame injustice ..."
>
>Glaucon: "Give me an answer to the following question about justice and injustice ..."
>
>Socrates: "...isn't injustice praised and justice blamed?"
>
>Glaucon: "Until I understand this, I won't be able to agree with you that justice is better than injustice ..."
>
>Socrates: "...consider what I am going to say next."
>
>Glaucon: "I'm listening."
>
>Socrates: "This, then, is what they say justice is like, and this is its origin and essence ..."
>
>Glaucon: "That's a good story, and it's very cleverly told."
>
>This question/response pattern continues throughout the piece.

Finally, the section from the Rebecca Goldstein interview that I had remembered (incompletely):

>**Reality**
>
>“I think there are things that are just, and there are things that are unjust, and I wouldn’t need a Socratic dialogue to show me which is which.”
>
>“We have to ask ourselves: How in the world did Socrates get so smart?”
>
>“Socrates is asking us to consider what reality is, and that includes what reality is in ourselves,” Plato would say. “So many of the deepest questions addressed in the dialogues are questions that are asked by each human being, in one form or another. What is the right thing to do? What is it to be a good person, and why should I bother? What is knowledge? What is love?”
>
>“But here’s the thing about reality: It’s real, even when we don’t see it,” Plato would say. “There’s an objective answer to the question, ‘What is justice?’ And there are objective answers to the questions ‘What is love?’ ‘What is knowledge?’ ‘What is the right thing to do?’ The answers to these questions exist, whether we realize it or not.”
>
>**Universal**
>
>“The Socratic method is not a game to confuse people,” Plato would say. “It’s a game to teach people that they don’t know as much as they think they know.”
>
>“Socrates knew he wasn’t as smart as the people around him thought they were, and he had to prove it to them,” Plato would say. “It’s not a trick to make you look stupid. It’s a trick to make you feel stupid. And that’s a big difference.”
>
>“The best way to help people understand the difference between what they know and what they think they know is to show them that their ideas don’t work,” Plato would say. “If you ask people to explain how they know something, that’s a powerful way to help them understand what they don’t know.”
>
>Plato would say that once you understand that you don’t know as much as you think you know, you can start to learn. He would say that’s the reason the Socratic method works so well.
>
>“As soon as you realize that you don’t know as much as you think you know, you’re ready to learn,” Plato would say. “You have to humble yourself to learn anything at all.”
>
>“The Socratic method is a universal method,” Plato would say. “It’s a method for all people, not just some people.”
>
>“The Socratic method is a method for teaching people to think,” Plato would say. “It’s a method for helping people to learn how to examine their beliefs.”
>
>“The Socratic method is a method for helping people to learn how to think for themselves,” Plato would say. “It’s a method for helping people to learn how to think critically.”
>
>“The Socratic method is a method for helping people to learn how to think deeply,” Plato would say. “It’s a method for helping people to learn how to think about the things that matter most.”

I think the way to use something like this is to copy a relevant piece into a notebook and juxtapose it with something that you are working on. If you are lucky, it illumines a bit both your ideas and the piece you are reading.

In this case, I think Socrates has an answer to the question of justice. We just aren't ready to hear it until we've admitted to ourselves that we don't even know what kind of an answer we are looking for.
