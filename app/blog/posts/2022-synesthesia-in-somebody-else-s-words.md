It is August, which means it's time for me to read about synesthesia again.  I haven't blogged about synesthesia since last August.

On Lightreading, a commenter writes:

> I don’t see colors when hearing words, but I do have associations between visual and auditory modalities.  For example, in the song Hunter by Björk, there is a riff with heavy reverb and it feels “blue” to me.

I don't think this is synesthesia.  How can it be?

If I said, "I don't have synesthesia, but I do have associations between certain smells and certain desserts.  For example, I associate the smell of smoke with ice cream.", you would consider that a perfectly sane thing for me to say.  It doesn't mean that I think smoke is ice cream, or that I hallucinate ice cream when I smell smoke, or anything like that.  Smoke just *reminds* me of ice cream, in a very strong, definite way.  (It really does, actually.  My mom smoked a lot when I was young and I really liked ice cream as a kid.)

Why would it be different for sounds and colors?  Presumably, the commenter above is not *seeing* blue when he listens to Hunter by Björk.  That would be synesthesia.  The way he describes it, it sounds like it *reminds* him of blue.  That is not synesthesia.  I don't see why the situation is any different than smoke and ice cream.

On the other hand, I think that you could be a synesthete and *describe* your synesthesia in the way he did.  If you don't know much about synesthesia, you might describe your experience as the sound *reminding* you of the color (or shape, or whatever), rather than *inducing* it.  I imagine the difference would be subconscious.  You don't think about *how* the sound evokes the color -- you just know that it does.  You might not even consider the implications of using the word "evoke".

If I were face-to-face with someone who wasn't sure if they had synesthesia, I'd ask this question:

"*Why* does this sound remind you of blue?"

I think I'd get different answers from someone who was a synesthete and someone who wasn't.  If someone answered with an aesthetic quality, like "The sound is mellow and blue is a mellow color," then I would conclude they don't have synesthesia.  If someone said something like, "I don't know... it just does", then I would suspect synesthesia.

Can you think of a better question to ask?  It's tricky because you don't want to suggest the right answer.  You can't ask, "Do you see blue when you hear that sound?"  And you can't ask, "Do you *think* of blue when you hear that sound?" because that is exactly how synesthetes are expected to think about their experiences.  They think that everyone's brain works that way.  They think that when they hear certain sounds, everyone *thinks* of certain colors.  They think that all people (or at least all intelligent people) have associations like that.
