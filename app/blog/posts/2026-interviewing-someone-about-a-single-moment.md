A while ago, I was writing about a project called [Elicit](https://github.com/sksq96/elicit) that I was working on. The idea was simple: take principles used by psychologists for interviewing people and use them to interview LLMs.

I’ve been in a deeply philosophical mood these days. So I’ve been thinking about Elicit again. Specifically, I’ve been thinking about how to do this manually for myself. So I’m reusing a lot of the notes I took for Elicit here.

I’d like to start by explaining the core technique from Claire Petitmengin’s work that I found particularly fascinating.

This is what she says:

> Although we are not familiar with the practice of introspection, we generally do have an idea of the meaning of this word, and we have the impression that we are able to do it. If you ask someone to describe their internal experience, he will tell you that he was sad, or anxious, or happy… Or he will describe his thoughts, his reasoning. He will explain to you why he did this or that, the reasons for his decision… He will tell you stories: he will describe what happened and the behaviour of the protagonists. He will tell you about his memories, his impressions, his ideas, his beliefs about such and such a subject. (…) But that’s not introspection.

She’s saying that when people actually try to introspect, they end up doing something much more superficial:

> Introspection requires a gesture that is counter-intuitive to our culture, which is generally very poorly executed, and it is the quality of this gesture that largely determines the quality of the information I can gather, and therefore the possibility of progressing towards a form of consensus.

The gesture is this:

> The introspection that we are going to talk about here consists of coming down one level, of turning our attention not towards the objects we perceive, nor towards the representations that we construct, but towards the activity that we carry out in order to perceive these objects, in order to construct these representations. It consists of becoming aware of the way in which we are aware. Turning our attention in this way is not easy, and generally requires a particular technique, a particular know-how.

She says that it’s far easier to do this with the help of another person. She calls that person an elicitor. Picture the elicitor as someone who holds a flashlight to the dark corners of your experience.

The first step is to ask you to find a specific moment. Not a genre of moment, not a representative moment, an actual moment.

When the Elicitor asks a subject to describe “how he proceeds to solve a problem”, “how he recognises a situation of hypertension”, “how he makes a decision”… he will have to invite him to find a singular moment in which this took place.

He could, for example, ask: “What did you do when, yesterday, you solved that particular problem?”

The author points out that the interviewee will often push back:

“I don’t remember”; “But I do so many things all the time, I can’t remember which one I did at that particular moment”; “It’s not in my character to solve problems like that, I’m not the right person”; “That particular moment wasn’t particularly interesting”; “But it’s always the same thing, I always do the same thing…”

The objections are real. They’re not resistance in the psychoanalytic sense, they’re the actual shape of memory. So the elicitor has to be patient, and keep offering handles:

> “What was that problem?”, “When did you solve this problem?”, “Where were you?”, “Who else was there?”, “Was it in the morning, in the afternoon?”, “What happened before?”…

And eventually, the subject will find one. You can feel it in their voice when they do. Here’s a sample snippet from an interview:

> - A: If you remember, what was your feeling on that day in November?
> - B: I was feeling a little sad, I was feeling a little sad, I was… I really felt a little lonely.
> - A: When you say a little sad, like that, can you tell me the moment, can you tell me the scene?
> - B: It was in the morning, I think, I was… I was in the kitchen, and then I was looking at the weather…
> - A: And what was the weather like?
> - B: It was a little cloudy, it was grey, I had already turned the heating up a little higher.
> - A: And what were you doing at that moment?
> - B: I was looking at the weather, I was looking at the weather, and I was listening to the kettle in the kitchen which was boiling, I was thinking, what am I going to do this morning?
> - A: And what could you see around you at that moment?
> - B: That morning I could see the roofs of the houses that were facing, facing my window in my kitchen, and I could see the birds flying over the roofs.
> - A: And at that moment, can you tell me, what were your feelings?
> - B: I was feeling a little sad.
> - A: When you felt a little sad, can you describe to me, perhaps, what happens in you?
> - B: When I felt a little sad, I was looking at the weather, I was thinking about what I was going to do this morning. I was thinking: I have to do this, I have to do that, I have a meeting, I’m going to be late…
> - A: And when you think about all that, what happens in you?
> - B: When I think about all that, well, I feel a little lonely.
> - A: And when you feel alone, what is happening in you?
> - B: When I feel alone, I’m thinking, I’m thinking, in my head I’m thinking, I’m thinking about my meeting, I’m thinking about my appointment, I’m thinking about the hole in the fridge, I’m thinking about the housework I have to do, I’m thinking about my neighbours, I’m thinking about the weather, I’m thinking about the tomatoes I have to plant in the garden.
> - A: And when you’re thinking about all these things, what happens in you?
> - B: When I’m thinking about all those things, I’m feeling a little sad and I’m feeling a little alone.

There’s a very specific rhythm to interviewing the subject.

> When the subject describes his experience, he often goes into too much detail, too quickly, and the Elicitor helps him to slow down. As soon as the subject has evoked a singular moment, the Elicitor invites him to stay in contact with this moment by asking him to describe what happens: “Take your time, when you have that moment in front of you, tell me what you see, what you hear…”.

This is a rather fascinating way to approach introspection. I wonder how the “quality” of my own introspection would change if I went down this path.

Anyway, here’s a fun thing you can try.

Pick some moment where you ended up doing something you didn’t want to do. Maybe you reached for the phone when you wanted to read. Or opened the fridge when you weren’t hungry. Or sent the message you’d told yourself you wouldn’t send.

Get your phone out. Open up whatever voice recorder app you have. Then read the following questions one at a time and try to answer them to the best of your ability.

Try to answer as though you’re talking to someone else.

I’m going to run through some examples with a fictional character I’m calling Maya. She deleted Instagram after losing two hours to Reels last Tuesday. She’s agreed to sit with a friend who’s going to ask her some questions.

The friend: Can you think of a particular time when you deleted Instagram?

Maya: Yeah, last Tuesday. It was, like, 11pm.

The friend: What was happening right before you opened the app?

Maya: I finished washing the dishes. I was putting my phone face-down on the counter and…

The friend: Wait. Before you opened it.

Maya: Okay. I turned off the tap. I dried my hands. I picked up my phone.

The friend: And then?

Maya: And then I… I was looking at it. And then I opened Instagram.

The friend: When you say “I was looking at it”, what was happening in you?

Maya: I was… I was looking at it. I was just… I was looking at the dark screen.

The friend: And did you say anything to yourself?

Maya: I… I… I didn’t. I don’t think I did.

The friend: How did you feel?

Maya: I felt that I didn’t want to be on my phone, I felt that I had to do something, but I was still looking at my phone, I was still… I think I was still looking at my phone.

The friend: What happened in your body?

Maya: In my body, I… I… I felt a little sad, I felt a little sad, and I felt a little anxious.

The friend: And when you felt a little sad and a little anxious, what did you do?

Maya: When I felt a little sad and a little anxious, I… I… I opened Instagram.

The friend: And what did you see when you opened Instagram?

Maya: When I opened Instagram, I saw reels. I saw reels.

The friend: And what were you feeling?

Maya: I was feeling a little anxious and I was feeling a little sad, and I was feeling a little…

The friend: What do you mean, a little…?

Maya: A little… A little… I was feeling a little… I was feeling a little alone.

The friend: And what did you do?

Maya: When I felt a little alone, I… I… I started scrolling.

The friend: And what happened?

Maya: When I started scrolling, I… I… I… I lost track of time. And I couldn’t stop. And I couldn’t stop. And I couldn’t stop. And I couldn’t stop. And I couldn’t stop. And I couldn’t stop. And I couldn’t stop. And I couldn’t stop. And I couldn’t stop. And I couldn’t stop. And I couldn’t stop. And I couldn’t stop. And I couldn’t stop. And I couldn’t stop. And I couldn’t stop.

The friend: And what happened after that?

Maya: After that, I… I… I looked at the time.

The friend: And what did you see?

Maya: I saw that it was 1am.

The friend: And what did you do?

Maya: When I saw that it was 1am, I… I… I deleted Instagram.

This is what the questions look like in practice. Notice how the friend keeps asking Maya to notice her physical sensations and her inner dialogue. Notice also how the friend never lets Maya get away with “I don’t know” or “I can’t remember.”

You could do this with a friend, or by yourself. I actually think this is a very powerful technique that you could use. In fact, I believe you could use this technique to achieve the status of an enlightened Buddha.
