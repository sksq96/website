I don’t like self-help content about depression because the people who can be helped by self-help content don’t need to be helped by self-help content. Most of my audience is too smart for this, but I want to be very clear: if you’re depressed, you should go to a doctor. They’ll ask about your symptoms and recommend medications, supplements, and lifestyle changes that are better than anything you’ll read on the internet. Ideally, you’d be open about this with a trusted friend or family member. They’re not going to solve your problems but they might be able to get you out of bed when you can’t.

That being said, I have some advice for people who are making content about depression. If your content is for sufferers, please don’t do this. You’ll get the tone slightly wrong and it’ll make them feel worse. If your content is for people who want to understand depressed people, great, here’s a model that may help.

* * *

The first thing to understand is that there are different kinds of depression. It’s a general term, like treating gout with a knife.

This post is about major depressive episodes, which look like prolonged (at least 2 weeks) bouts of lethargy, guilt, crying, distorted thinking, and uncharacteristic behaviors.

This is different from persistent depressive disorder (PDD, formerly dysthymia), which is more like a constant fog that lasts for years. PDD is a little similar to the fatigue you get from chronic stress.

Another kind is seasonal affective disorder (SAD), where a person gets depressed in the winter. SAD is the result of a broken circadian rhythm, and sunlight (or a light therapy lamp) can treat it just as effectively as antidepressants.

* * *

Now how do you write about depression? I’ll start by criticizing the worst kinds of writing and then show you some good examples.

The most common kind of bad writing is biography. Most people’s first exposure to depression is reading a biography of, say, Kurt Cobain. But since the biography is about Kurt Cobain, the novel, inspiring, moving, and exciting events of his life will be the focus. By the time the biographer gets to depression, you’ve forgotten that it’s supposed to be the bad guy.

The second kind of bad writing comes from *I'm OK, You're OK*. Everyone writes about depression as if it’s the same thing as sadness. And while they’re going to propose different solutions, they’ll all propose solutions that work for sadness. They’ll tell you to “think positively”, “go outside”, or “be grateful”. Some of these may actually be effective treatments, but they’re being proposed for the wrong reason and that will make you feel worse.

The third kind of bad writing is self-disclosing fiction. This happened to me just now. I meant to take a new example to illustrate my point but I got sucked into my old stories and didn’t realize until I’d written 200 words. I’m not saying my story is bad but it’s an example of the wrong *kind* of writing.

Depression is boring. It’s not interesting. It’s an enemy of the imagination. I’m writing this on a good day and I feel like I have to claw every word out of my skull.

You can make an interesting story about depression. But I’m not going to tell it from inside depression. I’m writing about it when I’m not in it, and I’m writing to you when you’re not in it. When you’re feeling it, you’re not going to read about depression. You’re going to be obsessing over your own problems, or you’re going to be in denial about having them.

* * *

The best kind of writing about depression is [anthropology](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1994/03/14/ontological-hysteria).

Consider this: If you’ve never been sad, then you can’t imagine being sad. If you’ve never been happy, then you can’t imagine being happy. The same goes for depression. It’s easy to imagine joy, sadness, and even euphoria. It’s hard to imagine depression.

I think this is true of many experiences. [People dislike nostalgia](https://www.amazon.com/Nostalgia-Longing-Meaning-Civilization-ebook/dp/B00A10GNZE) because it’s one of the few emotions that can’t be explained. I’ve tried many times, and no matter how I explain it, the listener just thinks “that’s what it’s like to be happy/sad/euphoric!”

Depression is like this. I don’t expect you to understand it. Instead, I’ll explain it so you know when you’re talking to a depressed person.

* * *

Okay, so what is depression? It’s a disorder of the maintenance system. Your brain has a few big systems:

- We’ve got a sensory system for taking in information.
- We’ve got a prediction system for making predictions.
- We’ve got a motor system for taking action.
- We’ve got a social system for forming relationships.
- We’ve got a maintenance system for solving problems for the other systems.

People often misunderstand the maintenance system. They think it’s just for doing the dishes and paying the bills—the boring tasks that keep you alive. If that were true, then depression would be like sloth and gluttony.

But think of it like a coprocessor. The maintenance system is a place for the other systems to throw work when they’re confused.

* * *

Here’s an example. Suppose you’re in a fight with a friend. The social system comes up with a plan: you’re going to make this grand gesture that will rebuild the relationship. But something doesn’t seem right—you’re not sure. So you call the maintenance system: “Hey, could you check this out for me?”

The maintenance system looks at the plan. It sees that the friendship is in trouble and sending a grand gesture will probably help. But it’s also concerned about the other system: it knows you’ve been a little weird, and it doesn’t want to make things worse. The maintenance system is also concerned about your reputation. Your friend is angry at you, and the maintenance system knows that anger can make people irrational. If you send a grand gesture to an irrational person, they could get angry and smear your reputation.

The maintenance system is like a dad who wants you to wear a helmet when you ride a bike. It knows that the odds of a bike crash are low, and it knows that the odds of a head injury are even lower. But it also knows that you’re a teenager, and that teenagers are irrational. And the maintenance system knows that you’re going to be really upset if you get a head injury, so it wants you to wear a helmet.
