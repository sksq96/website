One of the most famous books of the 20th century, Hermann Hesse’s Siddhartha was an excellent read. Filled with timeless wisdom, it leaves behind a long-lasting impression.

While reading, I decided to make a small experiment. I chose a chapter from the book and tried to describe it purely in action, leaving out what the protagonist was thinking or feeling. I chose the chapter where Siddhartha listens to the river.

This is how the chapter would sound like:

 > Siddhartha spent much of his time with the ferryman, Vasudeva. He talked with him. He helped him. He listened to the river. Sometimes, he listened to Vasudeva. Vasudeva smiled. That was their routine.

That’s it. This whole chapter, when you look at it from outside, is an extremely boring and dull scene. It is almost a non-scene, an awkward silence. But this whole chapter is about what Siddhartha is learning and feeling from listening to the river. And this chapter is crucial for him.

During this chapter, I was also trying to see if any of the things that Siddhartha is learning could have been passed down by a teacher. Could someone have told him this? Could someone have condensed all of his learning from this chapter into a single sentence?

I don’t think so.

As I went on reading the chapter, I kept on thinking about this. The answer always was a no. The answer was always a no.

And then, I read the last page.

 > You will learn it, but not from me. River has taught me to listen; you will learn from it, too. River knows everything; one can learn everything from it. Look, you have already learned this from the river too: that there is no such thing as time.

I was so glad and I chuckled.

(Some more notes about my process of reading: I usually highlight the passages I like or would want to revisit in future. But while reading Siddhartha, I thought it would be boring to revisit the passages I marked. I would already know them. So instead, I marked the passages and lines I couldn’t comprehend. This way, I thought, in future when I reread the book, I would get a fresh experience. I am glad I did that.)

While writing this post, I thought of adding the passage I highlighted in the book. But it was nowhere to be found. It only had a single passage:

 > It would be wonderful if he had many children, if this strong, handsome, serene man, with silent laughter, with indestructible cheerfulness, would raise them, teach them, stand by them in their mischief; if he, the seeker, had found peace, if he, the pale figure, had found happiness, if he, the thirsty one, had found refreshment.

I don’t know why I highlighted this passage. I didn’t remember this passage at all. Goes to show how reading habits evolve.
