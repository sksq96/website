## part 5 - Rumi, Sung in Qawwali

It's no secret that I've been listening to a lot of qawwali lately. Yesterday, I was going through my YouTube history, and stumbled upon a video I'd watched in June, an amalgamation of Rumi and Iqbal.

The video was uploaded by SufiSoul, probably my favorite YouTube channel (first to monetize my views!), and the qawwali was sung by Fareed Ayaz and Abu Muhammad, both among my favorite qawwals (and Sufis, though I've not yet written about the latter).

The lyrics are as below:

> Ajab ye raaz hai, dil mein
>
> Hath kaun hota hai
>
> To yun to saare sanam hai Khuda
>
> Par aashiq kaun hota hai
>
> Nazre mila. Yun tune mere se, tujh mein
>
> Yun tune mere se, tujh mein
>
> Haqeeqat khojta hai.
>
> Yun tune mere se, tujh mein
>
> Haqeeqat khojta hai.
>
> Khuda ke ik payaam-e- ishq
>
> Samajhne ke liye
>
> Samajhne ke liye
>
> Samajhne ke liye
>
> Khuda ke ik payaam-e-ishq sirf
>
> Samajhne ke liye
>
> Ahsaan o fiyaaz o raham
>
> Hone ke liye
>
> Hone ke liye
>
> Hone ke liye
>
> Ahsaan o fiyaaz o raham sirf
>
> Hone ke liye
>
> Haqeeqat ka pataa
>
> Kis ko mila
>
> Jis-ko Khuda ka ishq-e-haqeeqi mila

The first five lines are from Rumi, and the remainder from Iqbal.

The Rumi part is easier to translate, and I found a translation online (I don't know Persian, and am not very good at Urdu either):

> What a strange secret the heart holds!
>
> Who is the hand?
>
> Although all idols are actually God,
>
> who is the lover?
>
> You looked into my eyes,
>
> and you look into me
>
> in search of the Truth.

The Iqbal part, which I'll translate myself (with AI assistance), is:

> To understand God's one message of love
>
> To understand God's one message of love,
>
> Is only to be gracious, generous, and merciful.
>
> Who has found the news of the Truth?
>
> Whoever received God's true love

The reason I'm splitting the two is because, while I can't understand the Persian, and it's pronounced slightly differently, the rest of the song is in Urdu, which I can read, and somewhat understand. Using the Urdu script, we can identify the Rumi part with the first two lines:

> عجبا کہ دل میں راز ہے
>
> دست کون ہوتا ہے؟

And Iqbal's part with the third:

> اگرچہ جمیع بت ہست خدا
>
> ولی عاشق کون ہوتا ہے؟

I'll not claim my translation is perfect; in fact, I have other translations of Rumi for the same lines that are different. However, I won't dwell on the details of the translation; what interests me is the music, and how it fits the words.

Rumi's mysticism is described as ecstatic, and this is reflected in the music. Fareed Ayaz sings the first two lines in high pitch, and then repeats them a little more quietly. The lyrics that follow are then sung in a lower, more measured way, in a melancholic tune.

And this is where I appreciate the way the qawwali is arranged; Fareed Ayaz is a lover, and we can hear it in his voice as he sings the second part. He knows that God is love, and knows that without love we cannot find the truth. The lyrics are not just sung, they're felt.

And then, when he sings the last line, he repeats "Khuda ka ishq-e-haqeeqi", and seemingly forgets the rest of the line. He sings "Allah Hoo" instead, focusing on the ishq e haqiqi, which is followed by the chorus of "Hoo", and the clapping that is characteristic of qawwali. And this is where I can feel the ecstasy that Rumi's poetry is known for, and have to pause and give in.
