I’m reviewing a video of a talk I found a few weeks ago, featuring Leigh Brasington and Shinzen Young, interviewed by Joey Fasano. These are notes on the parts of the talk which are enlightening.

The relevant passage is around the 50 minute mark in the video.

Leigh Brasington starts off by explaining why he felt jhanas are important to teach. In his words:

> Jhana is the carrot. It is the reward system.

It is a method to:

> get the mind to really really want to be focused in the present moment

It is:

> to create a peak state, where people have this moment of really being completely present. And they see it’s possible and they want to do it again.

He then goes on to describe what he thinks the initial jhana feels like. My paraphrasing:

> You take a pleasant feeling, either in the body, or in an image, an emotion, something pleasant. You focus on it, and you let it build. This is a tipping point kind of situation.
>
> When you get enough feeling, the mind will just automatically focus on it.
>
> The mind doesn’t want to let go of it.
>
> It has gotten enough of the feeling, that it doesn’t want to let go of it, and it just sucks attention into it.

My attempt to relate to this:

It’s interesting and concerning that he says it’s a feeling, because I am not aware of having experienced anything like that, at least in the last two years.

I can think of an event, which may or may not fit the description. That event happened during a sit, where I was feeling a lot of resistance to meditating, and I had a really strong aversion to the whole activity. I felt this in a way which was very similar to how I experience fear. I was introspecting on the fear, and it was a very strong feeling. I just kept up with it. Until at one point, I just felt this sense of lack of resistance. It was like the fear had collapsed. I didn’t feel like I had switched to a different object of attention. It was like the fear had dissolved.

In that experience, I don’t remember a feeling being the cause of a “tipping point”. I felt like I was able to focus on the fear because I was completely non-reactive to it. That non-reactivity was cultivated by the fact that I was firmly rooted in the awareness of the fear. I was completely present with it, and not trying to change it.

Looking back now, I’m not sure if I can say that I had the intention to focus on the object. This is because I don’t remember feeling like I had to focus on the object. It didn’t feel like focusing. It didn’t feel like I was performing a task.

The feeling of fear was the most mind-consuming object during that sit, and so it was natural for me to be present with it. I just observed it. I didn’t have to do anything. Not even focus. The object was overwhelming, and it was natural for me to remain with it.

The fact that it was a really strong aversion to the whole activity, and that I was introspecting on that, made the object even stronger. This is because there was nothing else to occupy the mind. It was an overwhelming object, and it was an overwhelming experience to be non-reactive to it.

I don’t know if I can say that the intention to focus on the object was born out of the feeling of fear, or out of the fact that it felt like the only option.

In any case, I don’t remember a feeling being the cause of a “tipping point”.
