### Background and motivation

One of my favorite papers to read at NYU was "BERT has a mouth, and it must speak" by Wang and Cho (2019). I was initially hooked by the title, but the content was just as fascinating. The idea was quite simple. BERT is trained to predict words in a text given surrounding context but is usually used as a pretrained model for downstream fine-tuning. What if we could use BERT to generate text? Someone could say that the way BERT is trained doesn't allow for that. BERT is trained using a masked language model objective where it predicts a single masked word given surrounding context. So if some words are masked and we want BERT to generate text, we have to do something like Gibbs sampling, i.e., keep randomly sampling masked words and keep doing this for multiple iterations to get a sample from BERT. In fact, this is the equilibrium approach we discuss in the paper.

In our work, we instead propose to use a non-equilibrium approach. The main idea here is that BERT is a collection of conditional distributions. What we do is to sample from the distribution induced by the product of these conditional distributions. We actually got this idea from the work of Bengio et al. (2014).

### Summary

We start by discussing BERT as a masked language model and show that there are two ways to view BERT as a generative model. First, BERT can be viewed as a Markov Random Field. However, this approach would require a doubly exponential amount of time to generate a sequence since it involves sampling from the partition function.

Instead, we propose viewing BERT as a collection of conditional distributions. We then show that we can sample from the distribution induced by these conditional distributions in linear time. We compare this with Gibbs sampling and find that the sentences generated by our approach are more likely to be grammatical.

### Experiments

We first compare the masked sentences filled by BERT and GPT-2. We find that BERT is capable of filling masked sentences in ways that are difficult for GPT-2. However, BERT does not perform well when it has to generate text beyond filling a single word.

We next compare the sentences generated by Gibbs sampling and our approach. We find that Gibbs sampling is more likely to generate sentences that violate the input context.

Lastly, we test BERT for gender bias on the WinoBias dataset. We find that BERT has gender biases similar to those observed in word embeddings.

### Conclusion

We show that BERT can be viewed as a non-equilibrium generative model. We show that this non-equilibrium view allows for generating text in linear time. We also show that BERT contains gender biases similar to those exhibited by word embeddings.
