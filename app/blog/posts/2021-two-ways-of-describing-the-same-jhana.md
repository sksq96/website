I read [this article](https://www.thoughtco.com/buddhist-jhana-meditation-449688) on Jhana recently. Jhana can be described as a deeply absorbed state of concentrated meditation and there is disagreement among meditators as to how to describe it.

This is how Wikipedia describes it:

> It is a state of blissful immersion in any of various uplifting spiritual states, attained by the power of the mind alone.

> The basic idea is that you fix your attention on some object, and then maintain that focus without interruption or wavering for a long time—five minutes, an hour, or more. As you do this, your mind becomes progressively more stable, calm, and clear. Finally, it becomes absolutely unshakable, like the flame of a candle in a windless room, and you enter the trance state of jhana.

I will note that Wikipedia is a mediocre source for this, but I think it works well for this particular point.

The Wikipedia article also has a "pleasure section":

> In the jhanas, there is no pain or distress at all. The experience is enjoyable from start to finish. Each level of jhana is marked by progressively more sublime types of pleasure, such as rapture, bodily ease, and equanimity.

> The jhanas are a kind of intense pleasure, and the pleasure is essential

These descriptions are from two different people, but it's fascinating to see these two different takes side by side.

To talk about the first description, the author is talking about how to get there (concentration) and then what happens when you get there (blissful immersion). He speaks of the uplifting spiritual states, but there are no details. I would get the impression from this that after a certain amount of practice I will be able to hold a flame of a candle in a windless room. I have no clue what this will feel like, but it's supposed to be enjoyable.

The second description emphasizes the pleasure part of the Jhana. The author expects you to know what bodily ease is.

I think if they are discussing the same thing, then a Jhana-experienced person might be able to read the first description and infer the presence of bliss, bodily ease, and equanimity, but a Jhana-ignorant person will not.

One of the complaints of the bliss faction is that the first description might cause the Jhana-ignorant people to wait for a candle to appear. I think this is a reasonable complaint.

I will rephrase the first description to be less ambiguous to the Jhana-ignorant person: "After a certain amount of practice, you will enter a state of intense pleasure and bliss. You will feel a bodily ease and equanimity."

I am not sure if this is a problem with people's ability to describe their experience, or it's just semantic: i.e., there is a difference in the threshold of bliss required to call it a Jhana. Or, it's a combination of both.

(I think it is also a problem that the jhanas seem to be so desirable that many people get very bad feelings about them and don't want to feel pleasure and bliss. I am slightly disappointed to see that my teacher now wants to [downplay](https://youtu.be/m6Z5uWqd8aE?t=250) the pleasure part of jhanas. I think instead, he should address the issue of people getting bad feelings about jhanas. But maybe this is a personal preference. I like to feel pleasure and bliss.)
