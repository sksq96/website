I read many books in August, but one book that stands out is:

- Barrett, Lisa Feldman. How Emotions Are Made: the Secret Life of the Brain. New York: Mariner Books, 2018.

There are many complex ideas of Barrett’s theory of constructed emotion that I don’t fully understand, but I’ll spend this post looking at one claim of Barrett’s that I found fascinating.

Her account of depression (from 9 locations in kindle version):

> Exciting new research suggests that major depression may be a kind of predictive failure, where your brain’s predictions are too inflexible to accommodate new sensory evidence, causing those predictions to run amok.

The above definition is a little abstract, so here’s an example to make it more concrete:

> Imagine that someone says to you, “I like your new haircut”. If you are feeling a bit depressed, you might interpret this statement as a compliment. A person with clinical depression, however, might interpret it as a sarcastic comment. The culprit is the brain’s default prediction, which is based on your past experience, that “people don’t like me” or “people are usually insincere”. The sensory evidence that the person likes this new haircut is inconsistent with the prediction, but because of the degenerative nature of our wiring, the sensation is easily made to fit the prediction. The person is thus sealed in a negative world, and so might withdraw socially.

In this example, the sensory evidence is that your friend likes your new haircut, and the prediction is that people don’t like you. Even though the prediction is incorrect, the brain doesn’t change its predictions. That is what Barrett means by “predictive failure”. The brain is inflexible in the face of new evidence.

This is a simplified example of depression, but even after a lot of thinking, I still can’t think of an example where depression is not the result of a predictive failure. At its core, depression is a false belief that the world is a certain way, even with evidence that the belief is false.

If you have a condition where you don’t believe that you can change it, is that depression?

I don’t think so. I think the predictive failure in depression is much stronger than that. Let’s say I have a severe case of depression and a friend asks me to go on a hike. I might predict that the hike will be bad. That prediction will prevent me from going on the hike. I’ll never have evidence that hikes can be good.

But let’s say I have a milder case of depression, and I actually go on the hike. Now I have evidence that the hike was fun, and I’ll change my prediction. I’m no longer making a predictive failure.

If you have a condition where you don’t believe that you can change it, I’d call that stubbornness or a closed-mindedness. It’s not depression. A big part of depression is your brain refusing to update its predictions in the face of contrary evidence.

I think there’s a lot to the idea of a predictive failure, and I’m excited to see how it develops.

But now I’m worried that I might be too inflexible in the face of sensory evidence to update my predictions about Barrett’s theory of depression. I’m eager to hear any counter arguments to the idea of depression as a predictive failure.

My favorite quote in this book was the following:

> I went to the gym to train my brain, but I’m not flexible enough to learn this new language. I’m inflexible. I can’t train my brain. I’m hopeless.

This reminded me of a story my friend told me in high school. When he was really depressed, he went to the library to find books that could help. He found a book on depression and wanted to read it but couldn’t focus on it. He concluded that his depression was so bad that it couldn’t be fixed. I’m pretty sure that my friend was making a prediction that he was hopeless, and when he couldn’t focus on the book, his brain easily made that evidence fit the prediction.

His brain was making a predictive failure.
