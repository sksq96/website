What would a theory of pleasure have to explain?

I want to make a bunch of notes on how to build a theory of valence, and this feels like a good start. I’ll go through a few specific cases to try and describe some of the features of the theory we are looking for, and what an explanation would look like.

So, here’s a working hypothesis: valence is the perception of symmetry.

I’m mostly going to use the word “pleasure” in this piece, but I’m using it exclusively to mean “positive valence”.

In the previous post of this series, we covered the history of this idea in the work of Plato. Today we’ll start to assemble a theory, and at a later time we can look at how it relates to modern psychology and neuroscience.

In the last post, we saw how Plato used a theory of pleasure to create a theory of aesthetics and politics. What we have today is a lot of data on valence, and a lot of applications of valence to specific problems, but I’m not aware of a theory that is used to explain valence. This is how the title of Kahneman’s book on the subject is Thinking, Fast and Slow, instead of Feeling, Good and Bad. There is a lot of theory behind what our thoughts are doing, and how they are doing it, but the same can’t be said of feelings.

This is surprising because valence is the one universal property of all perception. Every perception you have is either good or bad, and this is true of every species of animal that can perceive. The domain of valence is conserved to such an extent that we often talk about the valence of perceptions that we wouldn’t attribute to our own experience as valence. For example, it makes sense to say that a healthy cell is “happy” even though the cell probably doesn’t “feel” anything in the way that we do.

But what is valence? How can we build a theory of it?

This series is the result of a personal research project I was doing in 2018, so much of the development is from a personal perspective and for personal reasons. I wanted to know how to build an artificial mind, and I started by trying to understand the parts of it that I was most familiar with. The most familiar part of my mind is how I feel. I have direct access to my own valence, so if I want to understand what it is, I can start by understanding what my own feelings are.

I’ll describe some of the things we will want our theory of valence to explain. The first thing it will need to explain is the difference between pleasure and pain.

My first hint that there might be something to a theory of valence was in noticing the cases where feeling something positive was indistinguishable from feeling something negative. The first two examples that came to mind were the feelings of relief and of being tickled.

When you are relieved from pain, it feels pleasant. But when you are relieved from pleasure, it feels unpleasant. In either case, the relief feels the same. It’s like it doesn’t matter what is creating the valence, just that the tension is released.

A similar equivalence exists with tickling. When you are tickled, it feels pleasant and unpleasant at the same time. You’ll laugh, but you’ll squirm away. The feeling is the same, it’s just the valence that is different.

These two examples are hints, but they don’t yet tell us much. We’ll need to find something that is different between feeling pleasure and feeling pain. For that, we can turn to the example of kicking a soccer ball.

If you kick the ball too hard, you’ll feel pain in your foot. If you kick the ball too soft, you’ll feel pain in your ego. There is only one way to feel pleasure in kicking the ball, and that is to kick it with just the right amount of force.

This is one of the most important observations: a lot of pleasure comes from being in the right state relative to the environment.

The specific mechanism of the pleasure is also important. Consider the pleasure of a sustained chord in music. A single note can be pleasant, but sustained chords are in a class of their own. Intuitively, it feels like the pleasure comes from certain frequencies of sound being in resonance with each other, as if they are in harmony.

This shows us that sometimes pleasure comes from something being in harmony with itself.

This is a bit of a catch-all term, but it points to a wide variety of experiences that have a similar feeling. Another example is the pleasure of a good massage. It’s like the pleasure comes from your body being in harmony within itself.

Here’s another catch-all example: sometimes pleasure comes from a change in state. This can be a change in the state of the environment, or a change in the state of your body. Think of the pleasure of discovering something new, or the pleasure of taking a deep breath.

This is a feature of the dynamics of the valence signal. The dynamics are hard to describe, but it’s as if the signal is looking for a change in state. Once it finds a pleasant state, the pleasure fades. If it finds an unpleasant state, the pain fades. If we want a theory that describes the dynamics of valence, it will need to explain why the signal is always looking for a change.

This is closely related to the fact that valence is always relative to the state of the environment. The same note in a song can be pleasant or unpleasant, depending on the context. The same kick can be pleasant or unpleasant, depending on the state of your foot. This is a feature of the signal that we will need to explain.

Okay, that’s enough to start with. If we can create a theory that explains these features, we’ll be well on our way to understanding valence.

We’ll start with the hypothesis that valence is the perception of symmetry.

The purpose of this hypothesis is not to explain valence, but to provide a framework for explaining valence. We will use this hypothesis to generate two classes of examples:

1. Things that we would expect to feel valence from the hypothesis, but don’t.
2. Things that we wouldn’t expect to feel valence from the hypothesis, but do.

If we can find examples of the first class, then the hypothesis is wrong. If we can find examples of the second class, then the hypothesis is incomplete. If we can’t find examples of either class, then the hypothesis is likely correct.

This is a powerful tool for building a theory, and it’s one that we will use to build a theory of valence. It is the same tool that we use to build a theory of physics, and it is the same tool that we use to build a theory of economics.

In the next few posts, we’ll start to generate examples of these two classes.
