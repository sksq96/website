I first came across the attachment styles lens through the School of Life’s intro video in late 2020, and later through Amir Levine and Rachel Heller’s book Attached: the new science of adult attachment and how it can help you find – and keep – love. I liked it because it gave me a vocabulary for talking about relating and noticing my own patterns.

The crudest version of the theory claims that people come in 4 styles:

- Secure: you feel comfortable in relationships, and you’re good at communicating and managing conflict. Roughly 50% of the population is secure.
- Anxious: you feel you have to work extra hard to get love, and you’re hypervigilant for rejection. 25% of the population is anxious.
- Avoidant: you like to be independent and hate feeling “smothered” by your partner. You prefer shallow relationships and feel uncomfortable with emotional intimacy. 25% of the population is avoidant.
- Fearful-avoidant: you have a mix of anxious and avoidant traits.
This theory is of course wrong, as no such trivial mapping between people and love can be correct. I’d always steer clear of adopting any such lens so absolutely. However, I also appreciate the value of a good simplification, if it helps you talk about the world. I’ve found that it does provide a useful vocabulary for talking about certain parts of relating.

The vocabulary helped me notice when I use behaviors that are unhealthy, and it helped me have conversations about these unhealthy behaviors. Some examples:

- A friend and I kept having this recurring conflict where they’d get angry when I took too long to reply to their messages. Eventually, I learned about the concept of protest behavior, which is a behavior intended to get the other person to pay attention to you when you feel threatened. I learned that demanding attention is a typical protest behavior for anxious people, and I used this vocabulary to explain how my friend’s behavior was making me uncomfortable.
- The book Attached has many tactics for behaving in a more secure way. I learned from it how to communicate my needs in a clear, non-accusatory way.
- I’ve also noticed the vocabulary of attachment styles makes it easier to talk about these things with friends. There’s something about the aesthetic of psychological concepts that makes them easier to talk about than their alternatives. It’s easier to talk about attachment styles than to talk about “insecure” behavior or “clingy” behavior, which are more judgmental.
A friend asked me if knowing about attachment styles made it easier for me to figure out what they were thinking. For example, if they didn’t respond to a message, I could ask:

- Is it because they’re busy (secure),
- Or because they feel they’re texting too much (avoidant),
- Or because they’re anxious that I’m upset with them?
I’m not sure if knowing the theory helped me come up with these answers. But it did make me ask better questions. For example, before I knew the theory, I just assumed they were busy. It was the theory that made me consider the alternatives.

My experience with the theory has given me a different perspective on psychology. Before, I thought of it as a system for explaining why people are the way they are. However, I now see it as more of a vocabulary for talking about relationships. I think most of psychology is like that.
