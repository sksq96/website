I was recently reminded of a workshop I'd been meaning to watch for a while, and I'm glad to have come back to it. It's a series of talks from the Program in Philosophy, Neuroscience & Cognitive Science (PNC) at New York University.

For the purposes of this post, I'm not interested in the nature of consciousness; I'm interested in what **consciousness does**.

I saved the playlist here. Below, I'll go through one of the talks, try to tease out one of the main points the speaker is making, and then apply that point to a related question:

Can someone's behavior remain the same while their consciousness is different?

Let's start with the first talk.

## Ned Block, "Consciousness and the Functions of the Mind"

Ned Block is one of the world's most distinguished philosophers of mind and consciousness. He earned his PhD from Harvard and spent most of his career at MIT (and then NYU).

The talk starts with the question:

> **What does consciousness do for us?**

Ned breaks it down into five further questions:

1. What is consciousness?
2. How does consciousness relate to the other functions of the mind?
3. Do any of those functions co-occur with consciousness so often that we can use those functions as tests of consciousness?
4. If so, could we use those co-occurrences to test consciousness in non-human animals and AI?
5. How does consciousness relate to the brain?

Ned is an expert in each of these questions, and each is worth studying deeply. For the purposes of this post, I want to focus on the second question.

## Local and global architectures

The first distinction Ned draws in the talk is between two architectures of consciousness.

The **local architecture** is what happens in various local processing centres in the brain. The **global architecture** is what happens when local processes are made available all across the brain.

For example, in some scenarios, we have a sensory experience that's not available for conscious report. This can be because our attention is directed elsewhere. Therefore, the sensory experience is local but not global.

In Ned's words:

> Let's think of the brain as a collection of processors. [...]
>
> The global architecture, as opposed to the local architecture, is cognitive processes across processors. So that's where you have information available not just to a single processor but to the whole brain.

## The global architecture

What are the elements of the global architecture? Ned lists some possibilities.

- **Access**: Availability of information to a large number of processing systems, including language.
- **Workspace**: The information can be broadcast to a number of processors in the form of a global workspace, which is a kind of working memory.
- **Unity**: The information might be integrated into a single event.
- **Self-monitoring**: The information might be available to the self in the form of a self-monitoring process.
- **Reportability**: The information might be reportable by the individual.
- **General availability**: The information might be available for any task.

Ned argues against the second-last, reportability, being central to the global architecture. He uses the example of a slow-motion study of tennis players.

> A slow-motion study of tennis players showed that the players returned the ball when they were not conscious of seeing the ball, [...], and they played differently when they were conscious of seeing the ball.
>
> So it really does seem like consciousness makes a difference to the way they play tennis. [...]

In other words, the players' behavior changed when they were conscious of the ball, even though they couldn't report it.

Ned goes on to argue that the global architecture is what's responsible for deliberate, reason-based, rational actions.

> There's a difference between me driving down the road, and I suddenly find myself at the airport, and an hour has passed and I don't know why, and now I am going through the airport, and I know why I'm going through the airport, it's because I want to get on a plane and go see my mother. That's a conscious action. And the difference between me driving down the road and me walking through the airport is that I'm in control of myself in one case and not in the other case.
>
> Now, what is it to be in control of yourself? It's just to be able to use reasoning. You can use reasons to justify your actions, and the reasons are what guides you. In the unconscious case, you're not using reasons. You're just doing it. That's one way to think about it.

## The local architecture

The local architecture is about more direct processing of perception.

> I'll give you a particular example of a local process. [...]
>
> Perceptual grouping is the process of putting together the parts of objects.
>
> So, perceptual grouping is a very particular kind of process. It takes place in the first visual area and the second visual area. It's a local process.

Ned uses a number of examples to distinguish local processing from global processing. Here's one example that should be familiar to anyone studying cognitive science:

> if you look at the Ebbinghaus illusion, what you see is that the circle in the center looks different depending on whether it's surrounded by big circles or little circles. But if you measure the distance that people reach when they're reaching it to pick up the circle, it doesn't matter. They reach the same distance.
>
> [...]
>
> that means the information that your hand is using is different from the information that your conscious perception is using. It's a different kind of processing.

To put this all together in the simplest way, local processing is about raw perception and the processing that happens in a single region of the brain. Global processing is about the combination of those perceptions across the brain.

## *What does consciousness do?*

So what does consciousness actually do?

Ned has a number of suggestions for what the global architecture is capable of, giving us a credit line:

> we can credit consciousness with **using information**, **manipulating information**, and **combining information**.

How does this cash out in reality? Ned says:

> The use of information is best exemplified in the combination of perceptual information and background knowledge. So, for example, if you look at this picture, it doesn't make any sense unless you know about the Jewish religion. So you need to combine the information in the picture with information about Judaism in order to make sense of it.
>
> Another thing that is connected with the use of information is manipulating information, and that's best exemplified by what's called **subgoaling**. Subgoaling is where you have a goal, and you break it down into subgoals. And you have subgoals of those subgoals. And you keep breaking it down until you get to something you can do. Now, that is something that you can do consciously, but you can also do it unconsciously.
>
> And finally, combining information is probably the main thing that you can do consciously that you can't do unconsciously. You can combine information in lots of different ways. You can mix and match and do all kinds of things.
>
> Now, the key idea here is that the global workspace is where you can do these things. It's where you can use information, manipulate information, and combine information.

The key insight here is that consciousness isn't a thing, it's a process. And that process is what enables us to use, manipulate, and combine information.

## What about the local architecture?

Ned's answer is that the local architecture is about raw perception, and the processing that happens in a single region of the brain.

> the local processes are more like the kind of processes that you see in a computer, where you have a local process that's doing a particular job, and it's not conscious.

So the local architecture does have a role to play, but it's not the same as the global architecture.

## The hard problem of consciousness

The hard problem of consciousness is the problem of explaining why and how physical processes in the brain give rise to subjective experience.

Ned's view is that the hard problem is a problem about the local architecture.

> The hard problem is not about the global workspace. It's about the local processes.

**Why?**

> The hard problem is the problem of explaining why there's something it's like to be in a particular process. And the local processes are the ones that are doing the heavy lifting, and they're the ones that are doing the work. And so the hard problem is about them.

So the hard problem is about the local architecture, and the easy problem is about the global architecture.

## What does consciousness do?

I think Ned has given a good answer to the question of "What does consciousness do?"

It's about the global architecture, and the global architecture is about using, manipulating, and combining information.

## Can someone's behavior remain the same while their consciousness is different?

> Yes, if the global architecture remains the same, but the local architecture doesn't have full access to the global architecture.

To give an example, what if we altered the local architecture of a tennis player, but kept the global architecture the same?

We could do that by showing the player a ball, and then asking them to hit the ball, but we could do it in a way that they don't consciously see the ball.

In that case, the player's behavior would remain the same, but their consciousness would be different.

That's what Ned's work is really about.
