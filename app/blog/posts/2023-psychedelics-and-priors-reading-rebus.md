I’ve been taking some light notes/reviewing recent stuff I’ve read recently. I should probably be calling these ‘closure memos’ but whatever.

I’m going to start with this cool ambiguous picture. Tell me what you see in it:

My guess is that you’ll see a bunny, or a duck, or both. The bunny will probably feel a lot more sure to you than the duck.

I see a bunny most of the time, and a duck on occasion. The bunny feels like it’s more likely to be the right answer, but I don’t feel certain. I’m actually not sure that you’d get a duck at all if you didn’t already know; I’ve seen lots of people see the duck when they’re primed, and I’ve seen almost nobody see it if they’re not. If you tried someone who didn’t know with a lot of pictures like this, you could actually test that.

Now, you might ask: why does the bunny feel more real? The answer, in very rough terms, is that it’s more likely that I’m seeing a drawing of a bunny than that I’m seeing a drawing of a duck. I see way more bunnies than I see ducks, so when I see a picture like this, it’s likelier that someone who drew it was trying to draw a bunny than a duck.

I think you can feel this if you stare at the image for a bit, and until you feel like you can switch between a bunny and a duck at will. When you try to see the duck, you’ll find the bunny tugging you back a bit. It’s not hard to switch, but there’s a tension.

The bunny feels more real because it’s the more likely interpretation. It’s the more likely interpretation because, in rough terms, it’s more likely that someone who draws an ambiguous picture is trying to draw a bunny than a duck.

The authors of the paper want to talk about ‘priors’. I think of a ‘prior’ as a kind of ‘starting position’; the things I think are likely before I’ve seen anything. Once I’ve seen something, I can move away from that starting position, but I’ll tend to still be near it.

The authors of the paper want to talk about a ‘prior’ as the weighting of a prediction. If I’ve seen a lot of bunny drawings, I’m going to have a strong prior that a particular picture is a bunny, even when I don’t have a lot of evidence.

The authors want to talk about the way that psychedelics affect our priors. They hypothesize that we see the world less rigidly because we have weaker higher-level priors.

I think of this as a shift in some of my starting position. My priors for what I’m seeing, at the highest level of abstraction, are looser. I’m not as stuck with what I ‘usually’ see, because I’m starting from a looser set of priors about what I’m probably seeing.

That’s their hypothesis. What’s their evidence?

The paper points to a study that found that humans on psilocybin were less prone to *binocular rivalry*. Binocular rivalry refers to when you show a different picture to each eye; the eyes can’t blend the pictures, so you alternate between seeing them.

If you’re on psilocybin, you’re less likely to alternate. You’ll stay on one image, and hold to it more firmly. The authors claim that this is because you have weaker priors; the things that would normally cause you to shift views have less effect.

That seems like a good piece of evidence. It seems like a way to distinguish the ‘weaker higher-level priors’ thesis from ‘everything’s just noisier’, which is important; that’s the main alternative.

There’s also a paper that found that on LSD, people were more likely to be able to see the ‘other’ image; a duck where they’d normally see a rabbit. This is also interesting, but it’s less of a distinction; you’d expect that effect under ‘everything’s just noisier’, too.

The authors also point to a study where they showed people a series of low-contrast/noisy pictures of faces and objects, while they were on LSD and while they weren’t. This might test ‘everything is just noisier’ vs ‘weaker higher-level priors’, but I’m not sure.

It seems like if you have weaker higher-level priors, you’d be less likely to see the ‘right’ answer. If you see a noisy picture of a dog, you’ll probably be more likely to see the dog if you have strong priors than if you have weak ones.

It seems like if everything is just noisier, you’d be less likely to see the right answer, too. If you see a noisy picture of a dog, you’ll probably be less likely to see it if everything is noisier, whether because you have weaker priors or because everything is just noisier.

So, I thought the binocular rivalry bit was cool. I’m less sure about the face/object thing.

The authors also point out that people on psychedelics are more likely to be able to see the ‘other’ image in the ambiguous picture. This is also interesting, but I think it’s less of a distinction; you’d expect that effect under ‘everything’s just noisier’, too.
