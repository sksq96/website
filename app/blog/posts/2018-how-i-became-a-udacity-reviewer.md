I've been a Udacity reviewer for around a year now (started: Jan 2017, stopped: June 2018). Here I'll share some details on how I got the position and what it was like.

I started an online masters program at Georgia Tech while working full-time in November 2016, and so between work and study I had very little time for anything else for a while. I had been waiting to start Udacity's [Deep Learning Nanodegree](https://www.udacity.com/course/deep-learning) ever since its announcement in June 2016, and so when the break in my school assignments came up in January 2017, I jumped in.

![image](/assets/images/udacity_deep_learning.png)
## The Entry

The course had a project on bike sharing data, where you had to build a neural network, train it and then make it generalize well. I worked on my project, submitted it... and then spent the next few hours talking to friends and reading up on latest news while waiting for Udacity to grade my project. The project was taking forever for them to grade, but that was par for the course.

The next morning, I got a notification that my project had been graded. The notification, however, had something else in it. My project had been graded by a certain Allan Downey, who had also added a message at the end of his review: if I was interested in becoming a reviewer, I could reply to that review and let Udacity know.

This was incredible. I wrote back immediately and told Udacity that I was interested. They got back to me soon enough, with some documents on what was involved and a take-home assignment to see if I could review this neural net project properly. I did the take-home assignment, and a few days later I was a Udacity reviewer.

## The Work

The work was very straightforward. I'd log in to my Udacity reviewer dashboard, and look for projects that I could grade. Once I found one, I'd review it, award points to it and provide detailed feedback to the student on how they could improve. 

![image](/assets/images/udacity_reviewer_dashboard.png)

The work was obviously repetitive, and I soon wrote a Python script to do the most repetitive bits for me.

The tough part came with the increased work. The pipeline for new reviewers had been broken for a while, and so for June 2017, I was the only reviewer for all of Udacity VR Nanodegree and for one of the Deep Learning Nanodegree projects. The resulting review load that month was... high. 

Let's say it was high.

The next month, I was given help, but the experience taught me an important lesson: there was a bottleneck in the review channel which was impacting people's learning, and I needed to fix it. Over the next few months, I worked with a friend, Megan, to design a new pipeline for reviewers, and also mentored new reviewers to help them get up to speed. This increased the number of reviewers from 1 to 3, and meant that all new Capstone projects were graded within 24 hours.

## The Community
The community of reviewers was awesome. The reviewer Slack channel was filled with knowledgeable people who were always willing to help. I remember when I was grading projects from a new course on Tensorflow, and I hadn't yet studied it. I asked in the reviewer Slack channel if anyone had worked on the projects and I received a ton of help.

When I was grading Udacity VR projects, I also used to go to the reviewer Slack channel frequently, since I didn't know Unity and the reviewer community helped me out with that too.

## The Automation
I soon became one of the top reviewers for Udacity in terms of the number of reviews I did, and soon after that I was asked to mentor new reviewers. But the increased load brought its own problems.

For example, when mentoring new reviewers, I would have to look at reviewer submissions and check if they were good enough. I would have a look at their reviews, then look at the student's submission, then see if the reviewer's comments were consistent with the student's submission. This was inefficient, and I was wasting a lot of time.

So I wrote another script, which would check the reviewer's review and the student's submission, and then provide me with a link to the places where the reviewer had failed to give feedback. This saved me a lot of time, and was a big help.

![image](/assets/images/udacity_script.png)

## The End
A few months later, when I was getting ready to work on self-driving cars full-time, I decided to stop reviewing. I sent Udacity an email letting them know, and they asked if I would like to keep doing it on the side. I said no, and that was it.

It's been a great experience, and I'm glad I got to do it. It was a great way to learn, and I'm glad I got to help others learn too.
