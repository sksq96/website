I have a weird relationship with Aldous Huxley’s *The Doors of Perception*. I think I first came across it in 2016. I checked it out, was intrigued, but never read it. For some reason, I kept coming back to it. I saw it again in 2018. Then again in 2021. And again in 2023. And today, in 2025.

I’m not sure why I kept coming back to it. Maybe it’s that I kept seeing the book, and it kept sticking with me?

For some reason, I always had this image in my head of Aldous Huxley eating mescaline, and then walking up a hill and looking out over a meadow. And then thinking, “Wow”. In my mind, he was just like a typical psychedelicist, looking out at the world in awe.

But then I read the book, and it was like….a bit different?

There’s a line in the book that struck me: “I perceive these things as being not the proof of an immanent divinity, but as being in some sort of Muhammadan heaven.” As in, he’s not seeing the regular stuff as divine. He’s seeing it as something else.

He writes about seeing the flowers, and “What rose and iris and columbine confessed to my non-verbal mind was the idea of gratuitousness. It was gratuitous that they should be there.”. He writes about them as being “self-evidently divine” but that’s not to say that they’re proof of a divinity in our normal consciousness. “To see ourselves as we are, here and now, in all the infinite and ageless dimensions of reality, is to see things as God sees them.”

He writes about how the “inner light” that the Bible speaks of is the Light of Being, the splendor of that which is. “the event that occurs is a lighting up, within the soul, of something that is already there and is ordinarily obscured by the irrelevant clutter of the mind.”

He writes about how if he were to be in a situation which was suffering, that he would still see it clearly. He would see it as though he were in the presence of the Divine. He wouldn’t be changed by it.

There’s another line I really like. “Why, in spite of everything, life is still worth living.”

I found myself really thinking about the line “seeing things as God sees them” and what that means. I don’t think that he meant that there is a God that can see things, in the way that we can see things. I think he meant that there is a position on seeing things that is “as God sees them”.

I think this is an interesting point to make, and I think it’s something that I’m still trying to fully understand. I can’t really say I understand it. I’m not sure that I’ve seen things as God sees them. I’m not even sure you can say that you have, unless you’ve had a properly divine experience.

We can do our best to see things as they are. But I’m not sure that we can say that we’ve seen things as God sees them. And I’m not sure that we can say that we’ve seen things as they are.

I guess that’s something to ponder.
