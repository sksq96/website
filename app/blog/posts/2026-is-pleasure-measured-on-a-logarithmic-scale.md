On 2025-02-05 I searched for "pain.pleasure log scale," and I search for this every once in a while because I'm not sure the question's been settled.

Here's my best shot.

An ordinary 1–10 rating assumes that the difference between 2 and 4 is the same kind of difference as the difference between 6 and 8. That's plausible for a thermometer, because the intervals are defined before anyone experiences them. It's less obvious for the felt intensity of an experience.

You can make a case for the logarithmic scale by the simplest of means. Imagine the following 1–10 scale.

1 - rape
2 - murder
3 - death of a spouse
4 - broken leg
5 - traffic jam
6 - stubbed toe
7 - yawning
8 - waiting in a long line

Maybe you're thinking, "the spacing between the bottom three seems right, but there's too much emphasis on bad things in the top half."

What would a logarithmic scale look like? Suppose that physical pain has an objective measure called "user annoyance units," or UAUs, and that these units measure the objective intensity of a stimulus. (It does not follow that UAUs are a linear function of a physical measure, e.g. heat or pressure. The subjective response to a physical stimulus can be non-linear without being logarithmic.)

We can ask a subject to rate the pain on a scale of 1 to 10. The instructions are something like "rate the pain according to how intense it seems to you." We can then tabulate the results in a graph. What you'd see is a function with a steep initial slope that flattens out over time. I don't know how many UAUs it takes to move from 1 to 10. Let's say going from 10 to 20 UAUs raises the reported pain from 1 to 2. At that rate, it would take 100 UAUs to go from 1 to 3, 1000 UAUs to go from 1 to 4, and so on.

The key question is, do those numbers accord with our intuitions about pain, pleasure, and suffering? It's hard to think about this. For example, if you have a headache, you might be able to distinguish between a 4 and a 5, but it doesn't follow that "a 5" has a fixed meaning that's the same for everyone.

If the logarithmic scale is correct, then we should be able to see it in the data, e.g. if a person can correctly rate the intensity of an experience repeatedly, the relation between the objective intensity of the experience should be logarithmic with respect to the person's report.

I think that in principle it's possible to build a device that measures the objective intensity of an experience. For example, suppose that we identify an electrode as "the" "pain" "electrode." We could apply 1 million UAUs to this electrode and observe that the subject reports a 7, and then we could ask the subject to tell us when the stimulus reaches the pain level of a stubbed toe. Then we'd have 1 data point. Then we could repeat the experiment. Will the second data point fall on the same curve as the first?

By the way, this is a way of solving a problem that I've had trouble with for a while. I've wondered whether it's possible to determine how intense an experience is "objectively." I now think that we can do this by identifying electrodes and asking subjects to give us data for a curve.

(The problem I'd been trying to solve was figuring out what a "hedon" is. Is a hedon a "unit of happiness"? If so, how much happiness is in a hedon? Which, in turn, is a bad question, because it's like asking how much happiness is in a bucket. A better question is, what is a hedon?)

If we have a logarithmic scale, then the numbers have no significance. A "9" is both the same 9 as a "1" and at the same time infinitely more intense: it is more intense than a "1" by a factor of 10,000.

So the question equivocates between two things. You could say "log scales do not preserve ratios," and you could also say, "they do preserve ratios, because they don't have to preserve absolute measures."

But there's also this question: is it better to have 3 days of extreme suffering or 100 days of lesser suffering? Clearly the latter. But what if it's 100 years of lesser suffering? This is an even harder question.

I think that if you do this experiment, you'll find that people will correctly identify "the" "pain" "electrode." (I put those words in quotes because the words don't mean anything other than "the electrode that registers as painful.") The question is, what will they do? Will it matter if you offer them more money?
