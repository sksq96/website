The first page of Spinoza’s *Ethics* has the following definition:

> By God, I mean an absolutely infinite being; that is, substance consisting in infinite attributes of which each expresses eternal and infinite essentiality. Explanation: I say absolutely infinite, not infinite in its kind; for, of whatever is infinite only in its kind, we can deny infinite attributes; but whatever is absolutely infinite, contains in its essence whatever expresses essence and involves no negation.

This is the definition that Spinoza will be using for the rest of the book.

The definition of God is performing a lot of work in the book, even in the first few pages. For example, Proposition 16 states:

> From the necessity of the divine nature must follow infinite things in infinite modes (that is, all things which can fall under infinite intellect). Proof: This proposition is evident from Axiom 6. For from any given definition a certain number of properties必然 follow (by Proposition 10) from the nature of the thing defined, and the more the nature of the thing defined expresses reality, the more properties follow from it; but the divine nature has absolutely infinite attributes (by Definition 6), each of which expresses an essence infinite in its kind; therefore (by Axiom 6) from its necessity infinite things in infinite modes must follow. QED.

The axioms, definitions and propositions that are referenced here are:

> Axiom 6. A true idea must agree with its object.
>
> Definition 10. By attribute I mean that which the intellect perceives of substance as constituting its essence.
>
> Definition 6. By God, I mean an absolutely infinite being; that is, substance consisting in infinite attributes of which each expresses eternal and infinite essentiality.
>
> Explanation: I say absolutely infinite, not infinite in its kind; for, of whatever is infinite only in its kind, we can deny infinite attributes; but whatever is absolutely infinite, contains in its essence whatever expresses essence and involves no negation.

The logic of the proof can be written in regular English as follows:
1. If you have a true idea of a thing, the thing exists (Axiom 6).
2. From any given definition, certain properties follow from the thing defined (Proposition 10).
3. The more reality a thing has, the more properties follow from it (Proposition 10).
4. God has absolutely infinite attributes, each of which expresses an essence infinite in its kind (Definition 6).
5. Therefore, from God’s necessity, infinite things in infinite modes must follow (Axiom 6).

Throughout the book, the definition of God is used in very different ways than how we use the word.

If you think of God as being a being separate from all other beings, then you might think, “Surely God has to be separate from me?” This is not Spinoza’s definition of God.

If you think of God as being a personal being, you might wonder if God will be angry with you if you do something or if God will be happy with you if you do something else. Again, that is not Spinoza’s definition of God.

In general, if you think of any of the attributes of God that you think of in normal usage and you substitute them into Spinoza’s usage of God, you’re going to end up confused.

Spinoza’s definition of God is more like the definition of a variable in an equation. It’s a placeholder for something that has certain properties. It’s not a thing that you can point to and say, “That’s God.”
