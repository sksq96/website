In February I wrote “so I’m just realizing that dreaming is very close to jhana”, which is an intriguing statement that I want to take literally. With that task in mind, I found this article (What Are the Jhanas Anyway?) which talks about jhana in a highly approachable manner. I will be using this as a reference for jhana.

> You should embark on this journey with the right kind of curiosity, patience, and enthusiasm. If you expect instant results or if you feel like a failure, you’ll probably get frustrated and give up.
> 1. Sit comfortably. You can do this on a chair, on a cushion, on the floor, or on your bed.
> 2. Take a few deep breaths and relax. Feel the air moving in and out of your lungs.
> 4. Let go of words and concepts. Jhana is a nonverbal, preconceptual experience. As you start to relax into the meditation, let the radio chatter of your thoughts fade away.
> 5. Let go of thinking. Trying to manipulate experience with thought takes you away from the present moment. Don’t force the jhana to happen; instead, let it happen to you.
> 6. Notice feelings. As you relax into the meditation, allow yourself to notice any feelings (Vedana) that arise in your body.
> 7. Notice what you’re feeling in relation to the feeling. Is it pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral?
> 8. Notice the nature of the feeling. Is it joyful or tranquil? Is it subtle or intense?
> 9. Notice what your mind wants to do. Is it interested in the feeling? Is it trying to suppress the feeling? Is it trying to refine the feeling?
> 10. Do what your mind wants to do. If your mind wants to notice the feeling, notice it. If it wants to refine the feeling, refine it.

> Jhana and dreams are two distinct mental states that can occur during different stages of consciousness. While they may share some similarities, they are fundamentally different experiences.
> Jhana refers to a meditative state of deep concentration and absorption. It is a goal in some Buddhist traditions and is characterized by a sense of stillness, calmness, and heightened awareness. In this state, the mind is focused on a single object or concept, such as the breath, a mantra, or a visual object. As the meditation deepens, the practitioner may experience various physical and mental sensations, such as bliss, tranquility, rapture, and equanimity.

Dreams are often associated with the rapid eye movement (REM) stage of sleep, which is a period of heightened brain activity. During this stage, the brain is highly active, but the body is temporarily paralyzed to prevent acting out the dream.
> While both jhana and dreams involve altered states of consciousness, they differ in several ways. Jhana is a voluntary state that requires effort and practice to attain. It is often described as a state of profound stillness and clarity, devoid of thoughts and distractions. Dreams, on the other hand, are involuntary experiences that are often chaotic and unpredictable. They can be influenced by external factors, such as diet, medication, or environmental stimuli.

And here are some interesting questions:

- If we are able to talk about jhana in words, then how nonverbal and preconceptual is jhana?
- If jhana has a sense of unity and interconnectedness, how is it different than love?
- If “the broken mirror world could be a jhana”, does it mean that the world is jhana?

All in all, I think these questions are worth exploring if one is serious about whether they are in jhana or not. For this, I would like to observe a few more descriptions of jhana and compare them to my experience. And, this is my current experience with jhana:

- It feels like being in a vivid world.
- It is addictive.
- I’m able to choose where to place my attention.
- I can make decisions, think, and do things in jhana.
- It feels like a dream. (If someone were to tell me right now that I’m dreaming, I wouldn’t be able to tell the difference.)
- I don’t feel a sense of unity and interconnectedness. (But if love is an emotion, then I don’t feel that either.)
- I don’t feel bliss, tranquility, rapture, and equanimity. (But they all sound like emotions to me.)
- I’m not sure if it has profound peace and tranquility.
- I don’t feel a preconceptual state. (But I assume that one can be in a preconceptual state and still do mental work.)
- I don’t feel a state of profound stillness and clarity.
- I don’t feel devoid of thoughts and distractions.

A question I have is: if I were a survey taker and was asked about whether I was in jhana, would I be able to answer that question? If the answer is no, then how can someone be sure that they are in jhana?

So far, I think jhana is very close to dreaming.
