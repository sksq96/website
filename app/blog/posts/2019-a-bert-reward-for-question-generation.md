I started working on Question Generation(QG) when I joined Microsoft Research. The problem is straightforward - given a passage and an answer span, generate a question. The state of the art model of QG is based on a neural Seq2Seq model. The goal of the project I joined was to improve the quality of the generated questions by making the model more context-aware. 

*Not all the questions generated by QG models are equally meaningful. Given the passage and answer span below, both questions are answerable but the first question is mundane.*

(passage) The term of office for a member of the House of Representatives is two years, with elections being held in each even-numbered year on the first Tuesday after November 1. The term of office of a member of the House of Representatives begins on January 3 following the election. Each Congress lasts for two years; therefore, all House of Representatives seats are up for election every two years.
(answer) two years

Q: What is the term of office for House of Representatives?
Q: When does the term of office of a member of House of Representatives begin?

The above example is from the recently published QuQu paper. I am one of the authors.

**How does QG work?**

If you are familiar with neural machine translation, QG works in a similar fashion. A sequence-to-sequence model with attention generates questions given both the passage and answer span. The model architecture consists of an encoder and a decoder. The encoder is responsible for encoding the passage and answer span into dense vector representations. The decoder takes in the encoded representations and generates the question. The answer span is usually highlighted by surrounding it with special tokens. The model is trained using maximum-likelihood estimation or cross-entropy loss.

For simplicity, let’s denote the passage by X, answer span by A and the question by Y. Let θ be the parameters of the QG model. Then, the training objective is:

L(θ) = − log P(Y|X, A; θ) = − ∑t log P(yt|y1, . . . , yt−1, X, A; θ)

**Quality of generated questions**

The generated questions are of different quality. How do we measure the quality? Either through intrinsic evaluation or extrinsic evaluation.

Intrinsic evaluation is about using metrics such as BLEU, METEOR and ROUGE which measure the n-gram overlap between the generated question and ground-truth question.

Extrinsic evaluation is about using the generated questions in downstream tasks. Imagine we have a QG model that generates synthetic questions given a passage and answer span. We can use the synthetic questions and answers to augment our training data to train a QA system. A better QG model generates better questions and thus helps build a better QA system.

**Why is QG important?**

One application of QG is in education, especially for reading comprehension. We know that the best way to learn is by asking questions. We also know that reading comprehension, which is the ability to understand a passage, is an important skill for learning. If we can design a system that generates good questions from a given passage, we can help teachers reduce their workload and also help students learn better.

**Reinforcement learning for QG**

Most of the work in QG focuses on syntactic modeling. The generated questions are syntactically correct but may not be semantically meaningful. This is due to the limitation of the training objective which tries to maximize the probability of the next word while being ignorant about the overall quality of the question.

One way to improve the quality of the generated question is to use RL where we treat the loss function as a reward function. We can use metrics such as BLEU or METEOR as our reward function. The problem with this approach is that it is based on n-gram overlap which is a syntactic metric and not a semantic metric.

One of the recent works in QG uses QA models as question discriminators to give feedback signals to the QG model. The idea is to check if the QA model can generate the ground-truth answer given the passage and the generated question. The problem with this approach is that the QA model is static while the QG model changes after every update. Thus, the questions which are most difficult for the current QA model may not be the same as the questions which are most difficult for the next QA model.

**Improving question quality using BERT**

In our paper QuQu, we use BERT as a question discriminator to provide feedback signals to the QG model. BERT is an encoder-only architecture which is used for language understanding tasks such as QA, NER, etc. BERT is bi-directional and pre-trained on a large amount of unlabelled text. Thus, it can be used as a question discriminator.

The BERT model is trained on the SQuAD dataset in the standard way. The passage and question are concatenated and fed to the BERT encoder. The output of the BERT encoder is given to a linear layer to predict the start and end span of the answer.

We use the BERT model in a novel way to compute the reward for the QG model. We take the passage and the generated question, concatenate them and feed them to the BERT model. We take the output of the BERT encoder and feed them to two linear layers which predict the start and end span of the answer. The loss is computed as the sum of the cross-entropy loss between the predicted start and end span and the ground-truth start and end span. This loss is then used as a reward function to train the QG model. The reason we use the sum of the start and end span loss is to take into account the fact that the start and end span are related. If the start span is wrong, the end span is likely to be wrong as well.

**Experiments and results**

We conducted experiments on two datasets, SQuAD and NewsQA. We used BLEU, METEOR, ROUGE and CIDEr as our intrinsic metrics. We used F1 and EM to evaluate the QA model trained on synthetic questions.

We found that using BERT as a question discriminator improves the quality of the generated questions. The questions generated by the QG model trained using BERT as a question discriminator are more semantically meaningful and less syntactically correct. We also found that the questions generated by the QG model trained using BERT as a question discriminator are more difficult for the QA model to answer.
