Sasha Chapin wrote a great piece called *MDMA: Alone*. It is just how it sounds—he took a large dose of MDMA and stayed home alone.

Sasha writes beautifully about what it feels like to be alone on MDMA:

> What is the experience of being alone on MDMA like?
>
> I’ve heard people compare it to being your own best friend. It’s a bit more than that. It’s more like being a very good friend to yourself. The kind of friend who wakes up in the middle of the night, checks out the swelling on your abdomen, and says “Oh, buddy, that is looking a bit strained. Might want to get that checked out.” A friend who, when you’re playing cards, says “Haha, dude, your face when you’re trying to bluff is just crazy. You’re so bad at poker.”
>
> I’m only partly riffing here—this is the kind of stuff you might say to a really good friend. It’s a curious juxtaposition—friends are supposed to let you be yourself, but sometimes, a really good friend will tell you when you’re acting like a jerk. It’s only because they care.

I have never done MDMA alone, but I did read this article alone. I was on the couch in my old apartment in New York, after cancelling a brunch plan to go to my roommate’s birthday instead.

I was lying on the couch, with the early spring sunlight streaming in. I read about MDMA, the neuroscience of intimacy, and the neuroscience of self-observation, and their connection to one another. I read about how empathy requires you to identify with others, and how positive self-regard requires you to identify with yourself. I read about how MDMA can stoke these feelings of identification.

During this time, my roommates were preparing for a birthday party downstairs. I knew this because I could hear them. And I knew that I could go downstairs and join if I wanted. But I didn’t. I stayed in my room and read.

In the medley of sounds and sensations that comprise our lives, it is easy to lose track of what is happening. It is easy to miss epiphanies hiding in plain sight. My epiphany, in this particular moment, was this: there is no fundamental difference between feeling close with myself and feeling close with others. I could grant them the same empathy, the same acceptance, the same patience, the same honesty.

I went downstairs after my reading session. We had cake and friends and music. I talked to people and listened to them, and I was able to hear what they were saying. I was able to hear them. And I was able to tell them about my reading session, and my epiphany.

I don’t know if this reading session made a difference in my life. I don’t know if this story makes a difference in yours. But I do know that taking an hour to lie on my couch in the sun and read about MDMA alone was one of the best ways that I could have spent that hour.
