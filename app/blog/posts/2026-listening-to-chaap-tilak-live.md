I was at the Kerala Literature Festival (KLF). My friend, who was with me, was heading out and I asked them to meet me in a couple of hours, by which time I would finish the session that was to begin in a few minutes. 

It was the Coke Studio episode “Chaap Tilak” by Abida Parveen and Rahat Fateh Ali Khan. I have added that to my May history.

“Chaap Tilak” is a centuries old, popular Sufi song, written by a poet called Amir Khusro, who lived in Delhi. Sufism is a mystical branch of Islam and much of its poetry is about the poet’s love for God. The Coke Studio version is perfect, but the one I had heard most of the time was the live performance by the Pakistani Qawwali singers Farid Ayaz and Abu Muhammad.

Another Indian Sufi singer, Mekaal Hasan has a gorgeous version too, which I had heard some years ago. The image of them singing in a beautifully lit hall stayed with me.

But I hadn’t heard this song live before. The KLF version was superb. I might even say it was better than the studio version. It was a small group: singers, instrumentalists and the man who gets the rhythm going with a hollow pot. The singer, who seemed to also be a poet, spoke in the pauses between songs, in a quiet voice, which was often drowned out by the clapping of the audience.

I was seated in the front of the small hall. They were singing the main song, Chaap Tilak, and then the singer turned to the audience and said, “Chaap Tilak. What does this mean?”

And he answered, “Chaap stands for the sound of the anklets when one walks. Tilak stands for the red mark women put on their forehead, to signify they are married.”

It was a lovely explanation, and one he had no need to give. But it was an act of heart, one that set the room for the rest of the song. 

The song is about a lover who has taken everything away from the poet/lover. I wouldn’t be able to say what those things are, because I can’t understand the language. But I would be able to sense it. That’s the thing about songs like these. There’s a lot of repetition, and one can sense a lot of the meaning even when we don’t have the language. 

My guess is that he was saying that his lover has taken away his everything. His ability to walk (Chaap), his love (Tilak), his intellect (buddhi), and his patience (dhairya). I might be wrong. But it felt that way.

I was enthralled by the song. I don’t know how to describe it other than that. I have heard many songs and seen many films, but I can’t describe what this song made me feel.

I am making this point because I want to understand what it is to listen to a piece of music, like this, live. How can we capture the fact that this is a live performance? How is that different from listening to the same song on a device?

The best I can do is to say that this is a live recording. I am not going to put it on the YouTube channel. I will let the person who recorded it decide what to do with it. 

This is what I heard.

There is a refrain (the melody and the words) that is like a mantra. The singer starts with it and then the whole group picks up the refrain. Then the refrain fades out and the singer goes on, just one person singing. And then the refrain returns, sung by everyone. 

This repetition of the refrain, like a mantra, is deliberate. It’s got to do with the meaning of the song. But it’s also what captured my attention. The singer would fade out, and I would be listening just to his voice. And it would feel like I was listening to the voice of one person, my voice, the voice of all of us. He would sing and we would catch up with him. 

This is what I mean by listening live. I am going to try to write more about this. I have some ideas about what this is and I will try to write about it.
