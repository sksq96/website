In June, [Ann-Sophie Barwich](https://twitter.com/neurophilosophy), a philosopher at Indiana University, came on the [80,000 Hours Podcast](https://www.80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/ann-sophie-barwich-on-smell-science/) to talk about what she’s learned from studying smell, and why more people should be studying smell.

It was a fantastic conversation, and one of the most interesting I’ve heard in a while. I’ll highlight a few of the most interesting parts, and add some thoughts of my own.

## Is smell a sense, or a sense of memory?

> the olfactory system is not just a sense of smell, but it’s also a sense of memory.
>
> …
>
> Let’s say you smell the smell of creosote, and you go “Oh, it smells like creosote.” That is, in fact, not a memory. That is knowledge.
>
> You remember that this is the smell of creosote. But let’s say you smell the smell and you go, “Oh, it smells … familiar.” That’s not a memory either. That’s recognition.
>
> If you smell something and you’re like, “Oh, it smells like the kitchen of my grandma’s house that I visited when I was a child,” that’s a memory. That’s remembering this particular situation.

Is the smell of your grandma’s kitchen the same kind of memory as a picture of your grandma’s kitchen?

It certainly feels different. I struggle to conjure the smell of my grandma’s kitchen. If I imagine it, it’s a poor reconstruction — a composite of the smell of mom’s kitchen as a child, and the kitchen I imagine my grandma might have had, and the knowledge that there was a pot of coffee that somehow became part of my mental image of her kitchen.

But I imagine that if, instead of trying to remember what her kitchen smelled like, I went and smelled it, I would instantly be transported into a time and place.

What’s missing from this picture?

Is it the smell, or is it the memory?
