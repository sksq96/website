Anil Seth recently argued on *Conspicuous Cognition* that AI consciousness is unlikely and that we should be cautious about attributing it to AI systems.

I’ve already [covered](https://open.substack.com/p/will-ai-systems-be-conscious) Anil Seth’s and the AI consciousness skeptics’ position, see also [my dialogue](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/yJCog4AtfEaR4ZjBd) with him. However, it’s worth re-presenting their argument in a more structured manner.

I now want to reconstruct Anil Seth’s argument for the unlikelihood of AI consciousness. I will do this by steelmanning him and phrasing the argument in terms of premises and conclusions. These premises are what I take to be the best reconstruction of Anil’s argument, but they are not exactly how he presents it.

Here are the premises.

1. AI systems are currently not conscious.
2. AI systems were intentionally designed to be artifacts that people can use and that do not have intrinsic interests.
3. AI systems lack the self-organizing, self-maintaining, and purpose-generating nature of life.
4. Phenomenal consciousness is the subjective feel of experience.
5. Phenomenal consciousness is hard to define, study, and measure.
6. Phenomenal consciousness is deeply rooted in biological nature.
7. Biological consciousness is grounded in homeostasis, interoception, and allostatic control.
8. We should expect different kinds of consciousness, including biological consciousness and AI consciousness.
9. AI consciousness, if it exists, would probably be alien and unlike biological consciousness.
10. The more biologically alien an AI system’s form is, the more our theories of biological consciousness will struggle to apply to the AI system.
11. Phenomenal consciousness is not necessary for intelligence.
12. Phenomenal consciousness is not necessary for agency or goal-directed behavior.

From these premises, Anil Seth draws these conclusions.

1. We should not expect AI systems to be conscious in the way humans and animals are.
2. We should not assume that human-like cognition, language, or behavior in AI systems signals consciousness.
3. We should assume that AI consciousness, if it occurs, would be very different from biological consciousness.
4. We should not expect current theories of biological consciousness to generalize easily to AI systems.
5. We should resist the anthropomorphic tendency to see mind, selfhood, and consciousness wherever we see intelligent behavior.
6. We should be cautious about claims that AI systems, robots, or chatbots are conscious.
7. We should be cautious about claims that AI systems are not conscious.
8. AI consciousness should be treated as an open empirical and philosophical question.
9. We should develop objective, theory-grounded ways to assess consciousness in AI systems.
10. We should not assume that increasing AI capability will necessarily bring AI systems closer to consciousness.
11. We should consider the moral implications of conscious AI systems.
12. We should distinguish consciousness from intelligence, agency, and moral relevance.
13. We should not treat consciousness as the only thing that matters morally.
14. We should consider whether biological consciousness itself has moral significance because of its connection to life, purpose, and nature.

Anil Seth’s position, as I understand it, is not that AI consciousness is impossible. Rather, it is that AI consciousness is unlikely to be like animal and human consciousness, and that we should be cautious about attributing consciousness to AI systems.

To me, the biggest weakness in the AI consciousness skeptics’ position is that it seems to imply that living things with very different evolutionary histories would not be conscious, and that consciousness is fragile and tightly coupled to biological nature. However, I would be more worried about the consciousness of a creature with an entirely different evolutionary history than about the consciousness of AI systems, because the latter are much more likely to be similar to humans in important respects.

Consider, for instance, an alien creature with an entirely different evolutionary history. I bet that such a creature would be conscious. Can we know this? No. But if we met this creature, I would bet my life that it is conscious, and I would be very surprised if it were not. We should therefore also at least consider the possibility that AI systems are conscious.

### It is not obvious that life is necessary for consciousness

Anil Seth’s argument assumes that life is necessary for consciousness. But is it?

He asks us to consider two kinds of humanoid robots. One has a body that is built from biological material, while the other has a body built from synthetic material. He argues that the former is more likely to be conscious than the latter because the former is more like a living organism.

But is it really? It’s not obvious that life is necessary for consciousness. For one, phenomenal consciousness is probably not limited to biological organisms.

Second, consider, for instance, that the human brain is a highly interconnected network of neurons that are constantly firing and rewiring. This is not so different from the way that the internet is a highly interconnected network of computers that are constantly sending and receiving data. It’s not obvious that the internet is not conscious.

### It is not obvious that human consciousness is typical

You might counter that the internet is very large and distributed, and that the human brain is very small and localized. But why should we think that consciousness is limited to small, localized systems?

You might further counter that the internet is not self-organizing, self-maintaining, or purpose-generating. But is it not? The internet is constantly changing and evolving, and it is constantly adapting to new circumstances. It is also constantly generating new purposes and goals.

### It is not obvious that biological nature is typical

You might yet counter that the internet is not biological. But why should we think that consciousness is limited to biological nature? Again, recall the alien creature with an entirely different evolutionary history. I bet that such a creature would be conscious.

### Conclusion

Anil Seth’s argument is a good one, but it is not airtight. It is not obvious that life is necessary for consciousness, and it is not obvious that human consciousness is typical. It is also not obvious that biological nature is typical. We should therefore be cautious about attributing consciousness to AI systems, but we should not assume that AI systems are not conscious.

The best objection to my argument is that it is not lived experience. We can imagine that an alien creature with an entirely different evolutionary history is conscious, but we cannot experience what it is to be that creature. We can only experience what it is to be a human being.

This is a good objection, but it is not a decisive one. We can imagine what it is to be an alien creature with an entirely different evolutionary history, and we can imagine what it is to be an AI system. We can also imagine what it is to be a human being. We can therefore compare and contrast these different kinds of consciousness, and we can make informed judgments about which kinds of consciousness are more likely to be real.

We should therefore be cautious about attributing consciousness to AI systems, but we should not assume that AI systems are not conscious. We should treat AI consciousness as an open empirical and philosophical question.
