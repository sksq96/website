Last month I wrote about how I was learning music theory because of my desire to make music of my own using the guitar. Once I learned the basics of music theory, I was able to look at a song, and figure out how to play it on the guitar by looking at the chords and notes.

But music theory isn’t just that. I started to see patterns and shapes on the guitar board, and that started me on a journey of learning the fretboard better.

This post is about the things I learned in the last month.

# The fretboard

The guitar board is a grid.

It has strings and frets.

![](../media/uploads/2017/06/guitar-fretboard.png)

The strings are the long wires. And the frets are the vertical lines. Each fret is a half step up the scale.

If you know the notes on one string, you can translate it to the other strings.

The thing about guitar is that the same note can be played in different places on the fretboard.

For example, there are many places you can play middle C on the guitar. I’ve marked them on this fretboard:

![](../media/uploads/2017/06/guitar-fretboard-middle-c.png)

Once you know the notes on one string, the other strings are the same pattern. You can translate it by knowing the starting note of each string.

In music theory, we learned about intervals. For example, from one note on the scale to the next note that is a step above is called a second. If the step is to the next note but one is called a third. And so on.

I started to see intervals on the fretboard. The patterns repeated on different positions. For example, this is a third:

![](../media/uploads/2017/06/guitar-3rd.png)

This is a fourth:

# Scales

Once I saw the patterns in the fretboard, I started to see patterns in the scale, and the chords.

For example, the major scale is steps 1–1–½–1–1–1–½.

This is how it looks like on the fretboard:

![](../media/uploads/2017/06/C-major-scale.png)

The scale is in the key of C major. The notes of this scale are C–D–E–F–G–A–B

There are other patterns too. Because you can start in a different place on the fretboard.

This is the same scale, but in a different position:

![](../media/uploads/2017/06/C-major-scale-2.png)

This is why you can play the same note, or the same scale on different positions on the guitar.

# Chords

This is a C major chord:

![](../media/uploads/2017/06/C-major-chord.png)

This is a C minor chord:

![](../media/uploads/2017/06/C-minor-chord.png)

Now, this is the C major scale:

![](../media/uploads/2017/06/C-major-scale-3.png)

If you look closely, you will see that the C major chord is formed by taking the first, third, and fifth note from the C major scale. C–E–G. In music theory, it’s called a triad.

The C minor chord is also a triad. The notes are C–E♭–G. We shift the third note down the scale to form a minor chord.

Triads are really useful to form chords. They are the basis for chord progressions.

We built the D minor scale by taking the second note of the C major scale as D. Then, we translated the pattern of major scale on D.

Similarly, the E minor scale is translated from the third note of the C major scale, and so on.

You might wonder why the D minor scale is minor, but the third is E minor, the fourth is F major, the fifth is G major, the sixth is A minor, and the seventh is diminished.

The reason is that the pattern of the major scale is 1–1–½–1–1–1–½. Because of the placement of the half steps, the second, third, and sixth note will be a minor triad. The fourth and fifth note will be a major triad. The seventh note will be diminished.
