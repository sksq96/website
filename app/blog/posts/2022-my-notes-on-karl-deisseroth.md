A few days ago I listened to Karl Deisseroth’s interview (as part of Lex Fridman Podcast #274). I consider it to be one of the best interviews on mental health (among a short list of interviews with Dr. Bessel van der Kolk’s, Dr. Jordan Peterson’s, Dr. Peter Breggin’s, Dr. Gabor Mate’s and Dr. Thomas Insel’s interviews). I had a lot of thoughts about it, but also felt kind of overwhelmed and didn’t know where to start. Here is my humble attempt to make sense of it.

# What is it like to be depressed?

I decided to start with a specific example that Deisseroth described in the interview.

> So I’ve seen this many times where someone will be reporting not much of anything and maybe even pretty upbeat and then you look at a biological measure and there’s a lot going on. And for example, one patient comes to mind he was a bookseller. I’m not gonna mention his name, but he was a bookseller at a used bookstore and he was a friendly guy but he was tending to be a bit of a loner. Definitely had friends and he was an avid reader and he loved to listen to music. He was a bit of an intellectual but he was not looking to date, he was not looking to get married. That was part of who he was.
>
>And then after a few years, he started to notice some things that he thought were maybe just part of getting older or part of being a bookseller. For example, he was seeing and hearing things that he didn’t think were actually there, but it was really easy to dismiss that because there’s something it’s just a hallucination. And he had a pretty strong sense that was not actually there.
>
>And also he started to lose interest in his other passions like music and reading. But again, he thought of that as maybe he’s just a little bit tired or something. He started to notice that he was not gonna say he’s disorganized but he did notice that he started to be less organized and he had trouble making sense of his days. He had trouble making sense of the sequence of events that happens during a day. And then he came in, he was gonna come in anyway because he was due for a visit and had a few things on his mind, but I didn’t know what they were gonna be.
>
>And he was saying that he started to notice that he wasn’t making sense of his days and also that he was just less excited about the reading and the music. And he thought that maybe he was just getting older. Maybe he’s just having a midlife crisis or something. So he came in and I drew some blood and I did a few things like that and basically found out he had syphilis.

This example is quite interesting to me, because it’s quite realistic. A very high level of functioning, and yet a lot is going on.

I do like how Dr. Peter Breggin described the difference in brain activity between a depressed and a healthy person as “a dimmer switch that’s tuned down a bit”. If I was to find an equivalent description, a real life analogy of a depression would be a sunny day, but you only see it through a tinted glass. Colors are dull, and everything is bit more grey, but there is still color.

While I don’t want to turn this into a discussion of meaning and behavior, I think it’s important to realize that we have a tendency to attribute meaning to our behavior and life events (“I failed a test, which means I’m a failure”). And a lot of our meaning is created post-factum (“I missed an important flight, which means I’m a failure”). While there is certainly an element of meaning in our lives, and things like being more “organized” or being “excited about the reading and the music” are important (and lack there of could absolutely be a sign that a person is depressed or is experiencing other mental health issues), we have a tendency to get stuck in our heads and lose contact with what’s happening in our bodies.

If I were to ask a person who was experiencing depression “What does it feel like?”, the answer would be different from person to person. Here are some common responses based on my experience:

* I don’t feel much of anything. It’s like I’m a robot.
* I’m feeling numb.
* I’m feeling nothing.
* I can’t feel happiness. It’s like I’m not capable of feeling joy and happiness anymore.
* I’m feeling a lot of apathy.
* Everything feels kind of dull and grey.
* It’s like I’m looking at the world through a tinted glass.

(For those who are interested, more information can be found in this PDF of the 2021 Depression Across Cultures study)

When I was listening to Deisseroth, he mentioned that a lot of patients tend to not be aware of their own symptoms. I’m thinking that depression messes with the ability to understand what’s happening at the level of one’s body, so it’s quite common that a depressed person isn’t really sure why they are feeling what they are feeling. So, in a way, they would be aware of their symptoms, but might not be able to make sense of them.

# How can science help?

One of the things that Deisseroth is excited about is the possibility to have “biological readout of some kind of subjective experience”.

> You know, I’ve been interested in this question for a long time, and I think one of the deepest mysteries in neuroscience is how it is that an internal state that only we can know about can be mapped onto the physical stuff in our brain, and there’s different answers to that question.
>
>Some people would say that it is not, there’s not a physical substrate of that. And some people would agree with that, but they say that the reason there’s not is that everything is a physical substrate and so it’s just part of the physicality that we are.
>
>So that would be maybe one category of answers. Another category of answers would be that there’s something that only we can know about that doesn’t correspond to anything physical, and maybe that’s what we call a soul or something, or a spirit, we have some kind of spiritual part of ourselves. And then there’s another view that does think that there’s a physical substrate and that it can be quantified and that we can understand it. And I think that’s where most neuroscientists fall into and I certainly do, and I think that’s where most psychiatrists fall into.
>
>And so what we’re saying is that what’s happening in the mind can correspond to something physical and the science can help us here.

While I think Deisseroth make a convincing case with his example of syphilis and depression, I do think it’s important to remember that (1) there are many different kinds of depression, and (2) they can have a different root cause. One of the most common types of depression is the so called “situational depression”, and it’s often caused by some significant negative change in life. It is usually a temporary depression that lasts anywhere between 2 weeks and 4 months and goes away on its own. Other types of depression can be caused by a thyroid problem, a side effect of medications, a head injury, or a substance use disorder.

The other question that comes to mind when thinking about this is whether it’s necessary to understand what’s happening in the brain in order to help a depressed person. If syphilis caused depression, then curing syphilis would be enough to get rid of the depression. But if a life-long trauma caused the depression, then it would be hard to measure that in the brain.

# Other things to note

There are quite a few other interesting things that Deisseroth has mentioned, but I don’t have much to say about them yet, so I’ll just mention them here:

* SSRIs don’t work for everyone. I’ve seen this as well.
* SSRIs have been shown to help with depression but there is a lot to learn.
* **Depression/anxiety can be a signal.** I would like to add that sometimes a depression can be a sign that we need to stop or change something that we are currently doing. I’ve mentioned this in my blog post on what depression is, but I think it’s worth repeating.
* **There are different kinds of depression.** Besides the previously mentioned seasonal depression and postpartum depression, there is also premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD), and major depressive disorder (MDD).
* **Physical exercise can help.** My personal experience tells that it’s quite difficult to exercise when you are depressed, since it requires a certain amount of energy. However, I do find it to be a great way to increase energy and to improve mood (as long as you don’t overdo it).
* “There are structural differences in the brain between people who have depression and those who don’t”. I need to learn more about this, but it doesn’t surprise me that there are structural differences in the brain between people who have depression and those who don’t.

# What I wish I could have asked Deisseroth

Since I’ve only listened to the interview a few days ago, I’m still processing it. Here are some of the questions I wish I could have asked Deisseroth:

* What does it feel like to be depressed? (I think that understanding how the person experiences depression is important in order to understand how to help them)
* What’s the best way to help a depressed person?
* What would you recommend to a person who is depressed?

# What I’ve learned from Deisseroth

If I was to summarize one thing I’ve learned from Deisseroth, I would say that depression provides a unique opportunity to learn about the brain. In many ways, Deisseroth’s and Insel’s views are quite similar. Both of them think that mental health issues provide a glimpse into the brain, which can help us to understand how the brain works.

> And so I think it’s important to know that there’s so much that we don’t know about depression and so much we don’t know about mental illness in general, but it’s a window into the whole brain.

I think this is one of the things that makes Deisseroth’s interview so interesting. I’m both amazed and excited to see the work that he is doing, and I think it would be amazing if we could understand how depression (and other mental health issues) works. That said, I think it’s important to remember that science is a tool, and it’s important to use it wisely. We need to remember that there is a lot that we don’t know, and that we need to be careful about what we do know.
